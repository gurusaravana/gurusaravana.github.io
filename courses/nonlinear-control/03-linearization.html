Notes

Why linearize?

Almost every tool of classical and modern control (transfer functions, Bode and Nyquist plots, root locus, pole placement, LQR) needs a linear model $\dot x = Ax + Bu$. Real plants are nonlinear. Linearization is the bridge: near a chosen operating point a smooth nonlinear system behaves almost like a linear one, so we design with the linear model and use it only near that point.

The Chapter 2 deck lists three families of linearization:

  • Analytical linearization (this lesson): replace each nonlinear function by its tangent at the operating point, using the Taylor series.
  • Graphical linearization: read the slope of a measured characteristic at the operating point.
  • Harmonic linearization: replace a nonlinearity by the gain it gives to a sine wave. The describing function of Lesson 4 is the main example; statistical linearization and the dual-input describing function belong to the same family.

The tangent-line approximation

Near a point $x_0$ a smooth function is close to its tangent line:

$$f(x) \approx f(x_0) + f'(x_0)\,(x - x_0).$$

This is the first two terms of the Taylor series

$$f(x) = f(x_0) + f'(x_0)(x - x_0) + \frac{f''(x_0)}{2!}(x - x_0)^2 + \frac{f^{(3)}(x_0)}{3!}(x - x_0)^3 + \cdots$$

The neglected terms are of order $(x - x_0)^2$, so the approximation is good when $x - x_0$ is small, and how small depends on how curved $f$ is near $x_0$. For $f(x) = \sin x$:

  • near $x_0 = 0$: $\sin x \approx x$ (slope $\cos 0 = 1$),
  • near $x_0 = \pi/2$: $\sin x \approx 1$ (slope $\cos\frac{\pi}{2} = 0$),
  • near $x_0 = \pi$: $\sin x \approx \pi - x$ (slope $\cos\pi = -1$, and $\sin\pi = 0$).
A linear model is only a local model. Always say which operating point it belongs to, and check how far from that point the system will actually go.

Operating points and deviation variables

A point $x_0$ is an equilibrium of $\dot x = f(x, u)$ with constant input $u_0$ if

$$f(x_0, u_0) = 0.$$

A nonlinear system may have one, several, infinitely many, or no equilibria. The linear system $\dot x = Ax$ with $\det A \ne 0$ has exactly one, at the origin. An affine model $\dot x = ax + b$ (what the tangent line gives) has its equilibrium at $x = -b/a$, not at $0$, so it does not yet fit the form $\dot x = Ax + Bu$.

The fix is to measure everything from the operating point. Define the deviation variables

$$\delta x = x - x_0, \qquad \delta u = u - u_0, \qquad \delta y = y - y_0 .$$

Since $x_0$ is constant, $\delta\dot x = \dot x$, and in the new variables the equilibrium sits at $\delta x = 0$.

Linearizing a state-space model

For $\dot x = f(x, u)$, $y = h(x, u)$ with $n$ states, $m$ inputs and $p$ outputs, expand each component of $f$ and $h$ to first order about $(x_0, u_0)$. Because $f(x_0, u_0) = 0$, the constant terms vanish and

$$\delta\dot x = A\,\delta x + B\,\delta u, \qquad \delta y = C\,\delta x + D\,\delta u,$$

with the Jacobian matrices evaluated at $(x_0, u_0)$:

$$A = \left[\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}\right]_{n\times n},\quad B = \left[\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial u_k}\right]_{n\times m},\quad C = \left[\frac{\partial h_l}{\partial x_j}\right]_{p\times n},\quad D = \left[\frac{\partial h_l}{\partial u_k}\right]_{p\times m}.$$

Example: the pendulum

A rod of mass $M$ and length $l$ swings about a pivot, driven by a motor torque $T$. Gravity acts at the centre of mass, $l/2$ from the pivot, and gives the moment $-Mg\frac{l}{2}\sin\theta$. With moment of inertia $J$ about the pivot, Newton’s law for rotation gives

$$J\ddot\theta = -Mg\frac{l}{2}\sin\theta + T .$$

With $x_1 = \theta$, $x_2 = \dot\theta$, $u = T$, $y = \theta$ and $a = \dfrac{Mgl}{2J}$:

$$\dot x_1 = x_2, \qquad \dot x_2 = -a\sin x_1 + \frac{1}{J}u, \qquad y = x_1 .$$

This is in first-order form but it is not a state-space model, because of $\sin x_1$. With $u_0 = 0$ the equilibria are $x_2 = 0$, $\sin x_1 = 0$: the hanging positions $x_1 = 2k\pi$ and the inverted positions $x_1 = \pi + 2k\pi$, for every integer $k$.

The Jacobians are

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -a\cos x_{1,0} & 0\end{bmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1/J\end{bmatrix}, \qquad C = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}, \qquad D = 0 .$$

  • Hanging, $x_{1,0} = 0$: $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -a & 0\end{bmatrix}$, eigenvalues $\pm j\sqrt a$. The linear model is an undamped oscillator with natural frequency $\sqrt{a}$.
  • Inverted, $x_{1,0} = \pi$: here $\sin x_1 \approx \pi - x_1 = -\delta x_1$, so $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ a & 0\end{bmatrix}$, eigenvalues $\pm\sqrt a$. One is positive: a saddle, and the upright pendulum is unstable, as everyone knows.

Written out for the inverted position, with $\delta x_1 = \theta - \pi$ the angle from the upward vertical:

$$\delta\dot x = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ \dfrac{Mgl}{2J} & 0\end{bmatrix}\delta x + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \dfrac1J\end{bmatrix}u, \qquad \delta y = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0\end{bmatrix}\delta x .$$

If you only replace $\sin x_1$ by $\pi - x_1$ and keep $x_1$ as the state, you get $\dot x_2 = \frac{Mgl}{2J}x_1 - \frac{Mgl}{2J}\pi + \frac1J T$, which has a constant term and so is not of the form $\dot x = Ax + Bu$. Shifting to $\delta x_1 = x_1 - \pi$ removes it. (In the lecture slides the $A$ matrix of this example lost its layout in the PDF; it is $\begin{bmatrix}0 & 1\\ Mgl/2J & 0\end{bmatrix}$ as above.)

Example: tank level

A tank of cross-section $A_t$ is filled by an inflow $q$ and drains through a valve with outflow $k\sqrt h$ (Torricelli). The level obeys

$$A_t\,\dot h = q - k\sqrt h .$$

At a steady inflow $q_0$ the equilibrium level is $\sqrt{h_0} = q_0/k$. The partial derivatives are $\partial f/\partial h = -\dfrac{k}{2A_t\sqrt{h_0}}$ and $\partial f/\partial q = \dfrac{1}{A_t}$, so

$$\delta\dot h = -\frac{1}{\tau}\,\delta h + \frac{1}{A_t}\,\delta q, \qquad \tau = \frac{2A_t\sqrt{h_0}}{k} = \frac{2A_th_0}{q_0}, \qquad \frac{\delta H(s)}{\delta Q(s)} = \frac{\tau/A_t}{\tau s + 1}.$$

Numbers. $A_t = 1\ \mathrm{m^2}$, $k = 0.5\ \mathrm{m^{2.5}/s}$, $q_0 = 0.25\ \mathrm{m^3/s}$: $\sqrt{h_0} = 0.5$, $h_0 = 0.25$ m, $\tau = 2$ s, and $\delta H/\delta Q = 2/(2s + 1)$. Doubling the inflow quadruples $h_0$ and doubles $\tau$: the "same" tank is a different linear system at each level. This is why controllers tuned at one level can behave badly at another, and why gain scheduling exists.

When the nonlinear term is 0/0: l’Hôpital’s rule

Suppose we want to linearize

$$\ddot x = -\dot x + \frac{\sin^2 x}{x}$$

about $x = 0$. The term $f(x) = \sin^2 x / x$ is $0/0$ at the origin, so $f(0)$ and $f'(0)$ must be found as limits. l’Hôpital’s rule: if $g(0) = h(0) = 0$, then $\lim_{x\to0} g(x)/h(x) = \lim_{x\to0} g'(x)/h'(x)$.

  • $f(0) = \lim \dfrac{2\sin x\cos x}{1} = 0$, so $x = 0$ is an equilibrium.
  • $f'(x) = \dfrac{2x\sin x\cos x - \sin^2 x}{x^2}$ is again $0/0$ at the origin. Applying the rule twice gives $f'(0) = 1$.
  • A quicker check with series: $\sin^2 x = x^2 - \frac{x^4}{3} + \cdots$, so $f(x) = x - \frac{x^3}{3} + \cdots$ and the slope at 0 is $1$.

With $x_1 = x$, $x_2 = \dot x$ the linear model is $\delta\dot x = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 1\\ 1 & -1\end{bmatrix}\delta x$. Its eigenvalues are $\lambda = \frac{-1 \pm\sqrt5}{2} = 0.618,\ -1.618$: a saddle, so the origin is unstable.

Linearizing about a trajectory

The same idea works around a nominal trajectory $x^*(t)$ produced by a nominal input $u^*(t)$, not only around a constant equilibrium. With $\delta x = x - x^*(t)$ and $\delta u = u - u^*(t)$,

$$\delta\dot x = A(t)\,\delta x + B(t)\,\delta u, \qquad A(t) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\Big|_{x^*(t),\,u^*(t)},\quad B(t) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\Big|_{x^*(t),\,u^*(t)} .$$

The result is linear but time-varying. This is how trajectory-tracking controllers for robots and aircraft are designed (practice problems Ex 2.9 and Ex 2.10 below).

What linearization tells you about stability

Let $x_0$ be an equilibrium and $A$ the Jacobian there (Lyapunov’s indirect method, proved in Lesson 5):

  • If every eigenvalue of $A$ has $\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda < 0$, the equilibrium of the nonlinear system is locally asymptotically stable.
  • If at least one eigenvalue has $\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda > 0$, it is unstable.
  • If some eigenvalue has $\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda = 0$ and none is positive, the linearization decides nothing. Example: $\dot x = -x^3$ and $\dot x = +x^3$ both linearize to $\delta\dot x = 0$, yet the first is asymptotically stable and the second unstable. The frictionless hanging pendulum (eigenvalues $\pm j\sqrt a$) is another case: its stability needs an energy argument.
Linearization predicts local behaviour well when the equilibrium is hyperbolic (no eigenvalue on the imaginary axis). It never tells you the size of the region where the prediction holds, and it cannot show limit cycles, multiple equilibria or finite escape time.

Exercises

  1. Find the equilibria of $\dot x = x^2 - u$ for the constant input $u_0 = 4$, linearize about each one, and say which is stable.
    Show solution
    $x_0^2 = 4$ gives $x_0 = \pm2$. $\partial f/\partial x = 2x_0$, $\partial f/\partial u = -1$. At $x_0 = 2$: $\delta\dot x = 4\,\delta x - \delta u$, unstable. At $x_0 = -2$: $\delta\dot x = -4\,\delta x - \delta u$, stable.
  2. A pendulum with viscous friction obeys $\ddot\theta + 0.5\dot\theta + 4\sin\theta = u$. Write $A$ and $B$ for the hanging and the inverted positions and classify each equilibrium.
    Show solution
    With $x_1 = \theta$, $x_2 = \dot\theta$: $A = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 1\\ -4\cos\theta_0 & -0.5\end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}$. Hanging ($\theta_0 = 0$): $\lambda^2 + 0.5\lambda + 4 = 0$, $\lambda = -0.25 \pm j1.98$, stable focus. Inverted ($\theta_0 = \pi$): $\lambda^2 + 0.5\lambda - 4 = 0$, $\lambda = 1.76,\ -2.26$, saddle (unstable).
  3. Linearize $\ddot x + \dot x + \dfrac{2(1 - \cos x)}{x} = 0$ about $x = 0$.
    Show solution
    $\dfrac{1 - \cos x}{x}$ is $0/0$ at the origin. By l’Hôpital (or $1 - \cos x = \frac{x^2}{2} - \cdots$) it equals $\frac{x}{2} - \cdots$, so $\frac{2(1-\cos x)}{x} \approx x$. The linear model is $\delta\ddot x + \delta\dot x + \delta x = 0$: $A = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 1\\ -1 & -1\end{bmatrix}$, $\lambda = -0.5 \pm j0.87$, a stable focus.
  4. Linearize the Van der Pol oscillator $\ddot x - \mu(1 - x^2)\dot x + x = 0$ ($\mu > 0$) about the origin. What does the result say, and what does it miss?
    Show solution
    $A = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 1\\ -1 & \mu\end{bmatrix}$, trace $\mu > 0$, determinant $1$: an unstable focus (or node if $\mu \ge 2$). So the origin is unstable. The linear model predicts oscillations that grow for ever; it misses the stable limit cycle that the nonlinear damping creates at larger amplitude (Lesson 2).
  5. For the tank $A_t\dot h = q - k\sqrt h$ with $A_t = 2\ \mathrm{m^2}$ and $k = 0.4$, find the linear transfer function $\delta H/\delta Q$ at $h_0 = 1$ m and at $h_0 = 4$ m.
    Show solution
    $q_0 = k\sqrt{h_0}$, $\tau = 2A_t\sqrt{h_0}/k$, gain $\tau/A_t = 2\sqrt{h_0}/k$. At $h_0 = 1$: $q_0 = 0.4$, $\tau = 10$ s, $\delta H/\delta Q = 5/(10s + 1)$. At $h_0 = 4$: $q_0 = 0.8$, $\tau = 20$ s, $\delta H/\delta Q = 10/(20s + 1)$. Both the gain and the time constant double.
  6. Explain why linearization cannot decide the stability of the origin of $\dot x = -x^3$ and of $\dot x = x^3$, and decide it another way.
    Show solution
    Both have $\partial f/\partial x = \mp3x^2 = 0$ at $x = 0$, so the linear model is $\delta\dot x = 0$ (eigenvalue on the imaginary axis). Directly: for $\dot x = -x^3$, $x\dot x = -x^4 < 0$, so $|x|$ always decreases: asymptotically stable. For $\dot x = x^3$, $|x|$ grows, and in fact escapes to infinity in finite time: unstable.
  7. Linearize the two-state, one-input system $\dot x_1 = x_2 + u\,x_1$, $\dot x_2 = -\sin x_1 - x_2 + u^2$, $y = x_1x_2$ about $x_0 = (0, 0)$, $u_0 = 0$. Find $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$.
    Show solution
    $A = \begin{bmatrix}u_0 & 1\\ -\cos x_{1,0} & -1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 1\\ -1 & -1\end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix}x_{1,0}\\ 2u_0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}$, $C = \begin{bmatrix}x_{2,0} & x_{1,0}\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$, $D = 0$. The input and output both vanish to first order: the linear model cannot be controlled or observed at this point, although the nonlinear system can be. Linearization can lose structure.

Practice problems from the exercise compendium

From Exercises in Nonlinear Control Systems by M. Johansson, B. Bernhardsson and K. H. Johansson (Department of Automatic Control, Lund University), Chapters 1 and 2. “Ex 2.6” means Chapter 2, Exercise 6. Work from the compendium statement. The hints and short answers here are written for this course; full solutions are not reproduced.

  1. Ex 2.6 A two-state system with output feedback $u = -Ky$: find the equilibria for every $K$ and the character of the origin.
    Hint and answer
    Hint. Set both right-hand sides to zero. The factors $1 + x_2^2$ and $1 + x_1^2$ never vanish, so equilibria need $u = x_1$ and $x_1 = 2x_2$. For the origin, the Jacobian is $\begin{bmatrix}-1 & -K\\ 1 & -2\end{bmatrix}$.
    Answer. For $K \ne -2$ only the origin; for $K = -2$ the whole line $x_1 = 2x_2$. $\mathrm{tr} = -3$, $\det = 2 + K$, discriminant $1 - 4K$: stable node for $-2 < K < 1/4$, stable focus for $K > 1/4$ (locally asymptotically stable for all $K > -2$), saddle for $K < -2$.
  2. Ex 2.9 Linearize the ball-on-beam equation around the trajectory with constant beam angle $\varphi_0$ and the ball accelerating down the beam.
    Hint and answer
    Hint. Use $\delta x = x - x^*(t)$ and $\delta\varphi = \varphi - \varphi_0$. Differentiate each term: $x\dot\varphi^2$ gives $\dot\varphi_0^2\,\delta x + 2x^*\dot\varphi_0\,\delta\dot\varphi$, and $\sin\varphi$ gives $\cos\varphi_0\,\delta\varphi$.
    Answer. Because $\dot\varphi_0 = 0$ the velocity terms drop out: $\frac75\,\delta\ddot x = g\cos\varphi_0\,\delta\varphi + \frac{2r}{5}\,\delta\ddot\varphi$ (with $g$ the gravity constant of the compendium equation). This one happens to be time-invariant.
  3. Ex 2.10 Find a nominal solution for the given sinusoidal input and initial state, then linearize about it.
    Hint and answer
    Hint. Try $y^*(t) = \sin t$ and use $\sin^3 t = \frac{3\sin t - \sin 3t}{4}$.
    Answer. $y^* = \sin t$ satisfies the equation and the initial conditions. Linearizing $\frac43y^3$ gives $4y^{*2}\,\delta y$, so $\delta\ddot y + 4\sin^2 t\;\delta y = -\frac13\,\delta u$: a linear time-varying system.
  4. Ex 1.12 Is the given system locally controllable at the origin? Linearize and test.
    Hint and answer
    Hint. Compute $A = \partial f/\partial x$ and $B = \partial f/\partial u$ at $(0, 0)$, then the rank of $[B\ \ AB]$. The term $u^2$ has zero derivative at $u_0 = 0$.
    Answer. $A = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 0\\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}$, $[B\ \ AB] = I$ has rank 2, so the linearization is controllable and the nonlinear system is locally controllable.
  5. Ex 1.14 Simulate the pendulum of Ex 1.1 ($m = 1$, $g = 10$, $l = 1$, $k = 0.1$), find its linearizations numerically and compare with the hand-derived ones.
    Hint and answer
    Hint. By hand: $\ddot\theta = -\frac gl\sin\theta - \frac km\dot\theta$. Any simulation tool can linearize numerically (Simulink linmod, or Python finite differences).
    Answer. Hanging: $A = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 1\\ -10 & -0.1\end{bmatrix}$, $\lambda = -0.05 \pm j3.16$, stable focus. Inverted: $A = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 1\\ 10 & -0.1\end{bmatrix}$, $\lambda = 3.11,\ -3.21$, saddle. Equilibria repeat every $2\pi$, so they are not unique.
