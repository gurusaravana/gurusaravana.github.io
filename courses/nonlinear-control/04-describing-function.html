Notes

The idea: a gain for sine waves

The describing function method replaces a nonlinear element in a feedback loop by an "equivalent" linear gain: the best linear approximation of the element for a sinusoidal input of a given amplitude. Because the gain is allowed to depend on the amplitude, the method can predict effects that no fixed linear model shows, above all limit cycles: their existence, amplitude, frequency and stability. It is also called harmonic linearization.

The method rests on four assumptions:

  1. There is a single nonlinear element, and the rest of the loop is linear and time-invariant.
  2. The nonlinearity is time-invariant.
  3. It is odd-symmetric ($y(-x) = -y(x)$), so its output has no constant (bias) term for a sine input.
  4. The linear part is low-pass: it attenuates the higher harmonics produced by the nonlinearity, so that the signal $x$ arriving at the nonlinearity is close to a pure sine. This is the filtering hypothesis.

Definition

Let the input be $x(t) = X\sin\omega t$. The output $y(t)$ is periodic with the same period, but not sinusoidal. Its Fourier series is

$$y(t) = A_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(A_n\sin n\omega t + B_n\cos n\omega t\right),\qquad A_n = \frac1\pi\int_0^{2\pi} y\sin n\omega t\;d(\omega t),\quad B_n = \frac1\pi\int_0^{2\pi} y\cos n\omega t\;d(\omega t).$$

For an odd nonlinearity $A_0 = 0$. Keeping only the fundamental,

$$y_1(t) = A_1\sin\omega t + B_1\cos\omega t = Y_1\sin(\omega t + \varphi_1),\qquad Y_1 = \sqrt{A_1^2 + B_1^2},\quad \varphi_1 = \tan^{-1}\frac{B_1}{A_1}.$$

The describing function is the complex ratio of the output fundamental to the input:

$$K_N(X, \omega) = \frac{Y_1}{X}\angle\varphi_1 = \frac{A_1 + jB_1}{X}.$$

  • For a static, single-valued nonlinearity (saturation, dead zone, relay) the output has quarter-wave symmetry, $B_1 = 0$, and $K_N(X)$ is real and independent of $\omega$.
  • For a nonlinearity with memory (hysteresis, backlash) the output lags the input, $B_1 < 0$, and $K_N(X)$ is complex, with a phase lag.

Saturation

Saturation has slope $K$ for $|x| \le S$ and output $\pm KS$ beyond. For $X \le S$ the element is linear and $K_N = K$. For $X > S$ the output is clipped from $\omega t = \beta$ to $\pi - \beta$, where

$$X\sin\beta = S, \qquad \beta = \sin^{-1}\frac SX .$$

The output is odd with quarter-wave symmetry, so $B_1 = 0$ and $A_1$ is four times the integral over a quarter period:

$$A_1 = \frac4\pi\left[\int_0^{\beta} KX\sin^2\omega t\;d(\omega t) + \int_\beta^{\pi/2} KS\sin\omega t\;d(\omega t)\right].$$

The first integral is $\frac{KX}{2}\left(\beta - \sin\beta\cos\beta\right)$ and the second is $KS\cos\beta = KX\sin\beta\cos\beta$. Adding,

$$A_1 = \frac{2KX}{\pi}\left(\beta + \sin\beta\cos\beta\right), \qquad K_N(X) = \frac{A_1}{X} = \frac{2K}{\pi}\left[\sin^{-1}\frac SX + \frac SX\sqrt{1 - \left(\frac SX\right)^2}\,\right], \quad X > S.$$

$K_N$ starts at $K$ when $X = S$ and falls towards $0$ as $X$ grows: the bigger the signal, the smaller the effective gain.

A few steps of the Chapter 2 deck’s saturation derivation have slips: the quarter-period prefactor is written $\frac{2}{\pi/2}$ (it should be $\frac{4}{\pi}$, which the next line then uses); one line has $[\beta - \sin2\beta]$ where $[\beta - \tfrac12\sin2\beta]$ is meant; and equation (12) still carries a factor $X$ ($\frac{2KX}{\pi}$), which cancels when dividing $Y_1$ by $X$. The final result, equation (17), is correct and matches the formula above.

Dead zone

A dead zone of total width $D$ gives no output for $|x| \le D/2$ and slope $K$ beyond. With $\sin\alpha = \dfrac{D}{2X}$, the output is $K(X\sin\omega t - D/2)$ from $\alpha$ to $\pi - \alpha$ and zero elsewhere in the half period. The same integration gives

$$K_N(X) = K\left[1 - \frac2\pi\left(\alpha + \sin\alpha\cos\alpha\right)\right] = K\left[1 - \frac2\pi\left(\sin^{-1}\frac{D}{2X} + \frac{D}{2X}\sqrt{1 - \left(\frac{D}{2X}\right)^2}\,\right)\right],\quad X > \frac D2,$$

and $K_N = 0$ for $X \le D/2$. A quick check: a dead zone is the line $Kx$ minus a saturation of slope $K$ and limit $D/2$, so its describing function is $K$ minus the saturation formula with $S = D/2$. It rises from $0$ towards $K$ as $X$ grows.

The deck’s final dead-zone formula is labelled "for $X > S$". For a dead zone the condition is $X > D/2$; there is no $S$ in this element.

Predicting limit cycles

Put the linear blocks together as $G(s) = G_1(s)G_2(s)$ and set $r = 0$. A self-sustained oscillation $x = X\sin\omega t$ goes once round the loop and must come back unchanged. In the describing-function model this means

$$1 + K_N(X)\,G(j\omega) = 0 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad G(j\omega) = -\frac{1}{K_N(X)} .$$

This is the harmonic balance equation. Plot the polar (Nyquist) curve of $G(j\omega)$ and, on the same axes, the critical locus $-1/K_N(X)$ as $X$ varies. Each intersection predicts a limit cycle: its frequency $\omega$ is read on the $G$ curve, and its amplitude $X$ on the locus. If the two curves do not meet, no limit cycle is predicted.

For a real $K_N$ the locus lies on the negative real axis, so we only need the phase crossover, where $\angle G(j\omega) = -180°$.

Worked example: relay control of a third-order plant

An ideal relay with $M = 1$ controls $G(s) = \dfrac{10}{s(s+1)(s+2)}$.

  1. Critical locus. $K_N = \dfrac{4}{\pi X}$, so $-\dfrac{1}{K_N} = -\dfrac{\pi X}{4}$: the whole negative real axis, from $0$ (small $X$) to $-\infty$ (large $X$).
  2. Phase crossover. $\angle G = -90° - \tan^{-1}\omega - \tan^{-1}\frac\omega2 = -180°$ gives $\omega^2 = 2$, $\omega = \sqrt2 = 1.414$ rad/s. There $|G| = \dfrac{10}{\sqrt2\cdot\sqrt3\cdot\sqrt6} = \dfrac{10}{6} = 1.667$.
  3. Amplitude. $\dfrac{\pi X}{4} = 1.667$ gives $X = \dfrac{4 \times 1.667}{\pi} = 2.12$.
  4. Period. $T = 2\pi/\sqrt2 = 4.44$ s.

Is the limit cycle stable?

Treat $-1/K_N(X)$ as a moving "critical point" and use the Nyquist criterion. Suppose $G$ is stable (or has only poles at the origin, as here, indented as usual). Perturb the amplitude of the oscillation slightly:

  • If the perturbed critical point is encircled by $G(j\omega)$, the loop behaves as if unstable and the amplitude grows.
  • If it is not encircled, the loop behaves as if stable and the amplitude shrinks.

A limit cycle is stable if, as $X$ increases along the locus, the locus moves from the encircled region to the non-encircled region: a larger amplitude then decays and a smaller one grows, so both return to the cycle. If the locus moves the other way, the cycle is unstable: a small disturbance sends the amplitude away from it. (This is often called the Loeb criterion.) For the relay example, increasing $X$ moves the critical point left, out of the encircled region, so the predicted oscillation is stable, as the simulation shows.

Worked example: relay with dead zone

A relay with output $\pm1$ and dead zone $a$ controls $G(s) = \dfrac{4}{s(s+1)(s+2)}$. The plant crosses the negative real axis at $\omega = \sqrt2$, where $|G| = 4/6 = 2/3$.

$K_N(X) = \dfrac{4}{\pi X}\sqrt{1 - a^2/X^2}$ is zero at $X = a$, reaches its maximum $\dfrac{2}{\pi a}$ at $X = a\sqrt2$, and falls back to zero. So $-1/K_N$ comes in from $-\infty$, turns at $-\pi a/2$, and goes back to $-\infty$.

  • With $a = 0.3$ the turning point $-0.47$ lies to the right of $-2/3$, so the locus meets $G$ twice. Solving $K_N(X) = 1.5$ gives $X = 0.32$ (an unstable cycle: the locus enters the encircled region as $X$ grows) and $X = 0.78$ (a stable cycle).
  • The system starts oscillating only if it is disturbed beyond the unstable cycle, and it then settles on the stable one.
  • To avoid sustained oscillation, the peak gain must stay below $3/2$: $\dfrac{2}{\pi a} < \dfrac32$, that is $a > \dfrac{4}{3\pi} = 0.42$.

How far can the method be trusted?

  • It is an approximation. The predicted amplitude and frequency are usually within a few per cent when the plant is strongly low-pass (relative degree two or more, with the crossover well above the corner frequencies).
  • It can be wrong when the filtering hypothesis fails: a plant with a resonant peak at a harmonic, or a first-order plant. It may then predict a limit cycle that does not exist, or miss one that does.
  • When $G(j\omega)$ and the locus are nearly tangent, a small modelling error decides whether they cross, so the prediction is unreliable.
  • The method says nothing about transients or about other kinds of motion such as chaos. Check the prediction by simulation, as in the relay example.
  • Uses beyond analysis: the relay auto-tuning experiment (practice Ex 5.9) uses exactly this prediction to measure the ultimate gain and period of a process for PID tuning.

Exercises

  1. Find the describing function of the cubic nonlinearity $y = x^3$.
    Show solution
    $X^3\sin^3\omega t = X^3\left(\frac34\sin\omega t - \frac14\sin3\omega t\right)$. The fundamental is $\frac34X^3\sin\omega t$, so $K_N = \frac34X^2$: real, and growing with amplitude (a hardening spring).
  2. A saturation has slope $K = 2$ and limit $S = 1$. Find $K_N$ for $X = 0.5$ and $X = 2$.
    Show solution
    $X = 0.5 < S$: linear, $K_N = 2$. $X = 2$: $\beta = \sin^{-1}0.5 = \pi/6$, $K_N = \frac{4}{\pi}\left(0.524 + 0.5\times0.866\right) = 1.22$.
  3. Explain why the describing function of any odd, single-valued nonlinearity is real.
    Show solution
    For $x = X\sin\omega t$ the output $y = f(X\sin\omega t)$ has the same symmetry as $\sin\omega t$ about $\omega t = \pi/2$ (quarter-wave symmetry). Multiplying by $\cos\omega t$, which is anti-symmetric about $\pi/2$, and integrating over a period gives $B_1 = 0$. No phase shift means $K_N$ is real.
  4. An ideal relay with $M = 1$ controls $G(s) = \dfrac{8}{s(s+2)^2}$. Predict the limit cycle.
    Show solution
    $\angle G = -90° - 2\tan^{-1}(\omega/2) = -180°$ at $\omega = 2$ rad/s. $|G(j2)| = \frac{8}{2\times8} = 0.5$. $\frac{\pi X}{4} = 0.5$ gives $X = 0.64$. Period $\pi$ s. The locus leaves the encircled region as $X$ grows, so the cycle is stable.
  5. A unit saturation ($K = 1$, $S = 1$) is in a loop with $G(s) = \dfrac{K_p}{s(s+1)(s+2)}$. For which $K_p$ is a limit cycle predicted, and with what amplitude for $K_p = 12$?
    Show solution
    $-1/K_N$ runs from $-1$ (small $X$) to $-\infty$. The plant crosses at $-K_p/6$, so an intersection exists when $K_p/6 > 1$, $K_p > 6$ (which is exactly where the linear loop becomes unstable). For $K_p = 12$: $K_N(X) = 0.5$; solving the saturation formula gives $X \approx 2.47$, at $\omega = \sqrt2$. It is a stable cycle: saturation stops the unstable linear loop from growing without bound.
  6. Show that for the relay with hysteresis the critical locus $-1/K_N$ is a horizontal straight line, and say what this means for the predicted frequency.
    Show solution
    $-\frac{1}{K_N} = -\frac{\pi X}{4M}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - (h/X)^2} - jh/X} = -\frac{\pi X}{4M}\left(\sqrt{1 - (h/X)^2} + j\frac hX\right)$, whose imaginary part is $-\frac{\pi h}{4M}$ for every $X$. The locus is a horizontal line below the real axis, so it meets $G(j\omega)$ where $\angle G$ is greater than $-180°$: hysteresis lowers the oscillation frequency compared with an ideal relay.
  7. Why does the describing function method work well for the relay loop of the worked example but poorly for a relay controlling $G(s) = 1/(s + 1)$?
    Show solution
    $G(s) = 1/(s+1)$ has phase above $-90°$ at every frequency, so it never meets the negative real axis and no limit cycle is predicted. The actual relay loop chatters (switches infinitely fast) about $y = 0$, a motion the method cannot describe. A first-order plant does not filter the harmonics, so the filtering hypothesis fails.

Practice problems from the exercise compendium

From Exercises in Nonlinear Control Systems by M. Johansson, B. Bernhardsson and K. H. Johansson (Department of Automatic Control, Lund University), Chapter 5 (Describing Function Analysis, Limit Cycles). “Ex 5.7” means Chapter 5, Exercise 7. Work from the compendium statement and figures. The hints and short answers here are written for this course; full solutions are not reproduced. The compendium writes the amplitude as $A$ where these notes use $X$.

  1. Ex 5.1 Match four odd static nonlinearities with four describing-function curves.
    Hint and answer
    Hint. Read $K_N$ as an equivalent gain. Compare the slope of each nonlinearity near the origin with $K_N$ for small amplitude, and its slope far out with $K_N$ for large amplitude.
    Answer. A curve that starts at zero belongs to an element with a dead zone; one that starts high and falls belongs to a saturating or relay-like element; one that rises belongs to an element that gets steeper.
  2. Ex 5.2 Describing functions of the saturation, the dead zone and a piecewise-linear function.
    Hint and answer
    Hint. Derive the saturation first. Then write the dead zone as "line minus saturation" and the piecewise-linear function as a sum of saturations with different limits and slopes.
    Answer. Saturation as in the notes. Dead zone $= K - K_{N,\text{sat}}$. A piecewise-linear function with breakpoints $\alpha$ and $\beta$ is a weighted sum of two saturation describing functions.
  3. Ex 5.3 Show the given formula for $-1/N(A)$ of a relay with hysteresis.
    Hint and answer
    Hint. Find where the output switches in the cycle ($A\sin\omega t = \pm D$), compute $A_1$ and $B_1$, then invert.
    Answer. The locus is the horizontal line $\mathrm{Im} = -\frac{\pi D}{4H}$ (compendium notation: switching level $D$, output $H$), with real part $-\frac{\pi}{4H}\sqrt{A^2 - D^2}$.
  4. Ex 5.4 Relate the describing function of $D f(x/D)$ to that of $f(x)$.
    Hint and answer
    Hint. Substitute the input $C\sin\omega t$ and change variables in the Fourier integral.
    Answer. It equals $Y_N(C/D)$: scaling input and output by the same factor only rescales the amplitude axis.
  5. Ex 5.6 Describing function of $f(x) = k_1x + k_2x^2 + k_3x^3$; the role of $k_2x^2$.
    Hint and answer
    Hint. Use $\sin^2\theta = \frac{1 - \cos2\theta}{2}$ and $\sin^3\theta = \frac{3\sin\theta - \sin3\theta}{4}$.
    Answer. $N(A) = k_1 + \frac34k_3A^2$. The even term $k_2x^2$ gives only a bias and a second harmonic, no fundamental, so it does not enter $N$; but it breaks the odd-symmetry assumption, and the bias it creates can shift the operating point.
  6. Ex 5.7 An electronic oscillator: saturation in a loop with $G(s) = \dfrac{-5s}{s^2 + s + 25}$. Is there a limit cycle, with what frequency and amplitude, and is it stable?
    Hint and answer
    Hint. Split $G(j\omega)$ into real and imaginary parts (multiply by the conjugate of the denominator). Find where it is real and negative.
    Answer. $G(j5) = -5$, so $\omega = 5$ rad/s and $N(A) = 0.2$. For a unit saturation this gives $A \approx 6.3$. The locus leaves the encircled region as $A$ grows: a stable limit cycle, which is what makes the circuit a good oscillator.
  7. Ex 5.8 A servo $\dfrac{4}{s(s+1)(s+2)}$ with a relay with dead zone $a$: show the describing function and choose $a$ to avoid oscillation.
    Hint and answer
    Hint. For (a) integrate the pulse output between the switching angles. For (b) find the maximum of $N(A)$ and compare it with the gain margin of $G_0$.
    Answer. $N$ peaks at $\frac{2}{\pi a}$ when $A = a\sqrt2$; $G_0$ crosses the real axis at $-2/3$. No oscillation if $\frac{2}{\pi a} < \frac32$, i.e. $a > \frac{4}{3\pi} \approx 0.42$ (the worked example in the notes).
  8. Ex 5.9 Relay auto-tuning: estimate the ultimate gain and period from the relay oscillation, and apply it to $G(s) = \dfrac{50}{s(s+1)(s+10)}$.
    Hint and answer
    Hint. At the oscillation, $K_N(A)\,|G(j\omega_u)| = 1$ with $K_N = \frac{4D}{\pi A}$.
    Answer. $K_u \approx \frac{4D}{\pi A}$, $T_u = \frac{2\pi}{\omega}$. For this plant $\omega_u = \sqrt{10}$, $|G(j\omega_u)| = 0.455$, so the relay gives $A \approx 0.58$ (for $D = 1$), $K_u \approx 2.2$ and $T_u \approx 1.99$ s, matching the analytical values.
  9. Ex 5.12 Show that $G(s) = \dfrac{1}{s(s+1)^2}$ with relay feedback has a locally stable limit cycle.
    Hint and answer
    Hint. Find the phase crossover of $G$ and apply the stability rule for the relay locus.
    Answer. $\angle G = -180°$ at $\omega = 1$, $|G(j1)| = 0.5$, so $A = \frac{4 \times 0.5}{\pi} = 0.64$ for a unit relay, period $2\pi$. The locus leaves the encircled region as $A$ grows: stable. (A rigorous proof uses the Poincaré map of Ex 5.13.)
