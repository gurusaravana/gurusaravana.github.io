Notes

What stability means

  • A linear, time-invariant system is stable if its natural response decays to zero as time goes to infinity.
  • It is unstable if the natural response grows without bound.
  • It is marginally stable if the natural response neither decays nor grows, but stays constant or oscillates.
  • BIBO stability: every bounded input gives a bounded output.

In terms of poles: a system is stable when every closed-loop pole lies in the left half of the s-plane (negative real part). Any pole in the right half-plane makes it unstable; simple (non-repeated) poles on the $j\omega$ axis give marginal stability.

The Routh–Hurwitz criterion

The number of roots of the characteristic equation with positive real parts equals the number of sign changes in the first column of the Routh array. We learn how many poles are unstable without solving for them.

For $a_0s^n + a_1s^{n-1} + \dots + a_n = 0$, write the first two rows from alternate coefficients, then

$$b_1 = \frac{a_1a_2 - a_0a_3}{a_1},\qquad b_2 = \frac{a_1a_4 - a_0a_5}{a_1},\ \dots$$

A whole row may be multiplied or divided by a positive number to keep the arithmetic simple; this does not change any signs. A quick first check: if any coefficient is missing or has a different sign from the others, the system cannot be stable.

Special cases

  • Zero in the first column (rest of the row not zero): replace it with a small $\varepsilon > 0$, finish the array, then let $\varepsilon \to 0^+$.
  • Whole row of zeros: there are roots placed symmetrically about the origin (for example a pair on the $j\omega$ axis). Form the auxiliary polynomial $A(s)$ from the row above, replace the zero row with the coefficients of $dA/ds$, and continue. The roots of $A(s)$ are among the system's roots.

Range of $K$

Keep $K$ as a symbol, require every first-column entry to be positive, and solve. The boundary value is the marginal (critical) gain; the auxiliary equation then gives the frequency of oscillation. This is exactly how the Ziegler–Nichols critical gain was found in Lesson 10.

Exercises

  1. Is $s^3 + 2s^2 + 3s + 10 = 0$ stable?
    Show solution
    First column: 1, 2, $(6 - 10)/2 = -2$, 10. Two sign changes: unstable with two right-half-plane roots.
  2. Find $K$ for stability: $s^3 + 3s^2 + 2s + K = 0$.
    Show solution
    $s^1$ row: $(6 - K)/3$. Need $0 < K < 6$. At $K = 6$: $3s^2 + 6 = 0$, so it oscillates at $\omega = \sqrt2$ rad/s.
  3. What does a row of zeros tell you?
    Show solution
    There are roots placed symmetrically about the origin, such as a pair on the imaginary axis, found from the auxiliary polynomial.
  4. How many roots of $s^4 + 2s^3 + 3s^2 + 4s + 5 = 0$ lie in the right half-plane?
    Show solution
    Rows: $s^4$: 1, 3, 5; $s^3$: 2, 4; $s^2$: 1, 5; $s^1$: $(4 - 10)/1 = -6$; $s^0$: 5. First column $1, 2, 1, -6, 5$: two sign changes, so two RHP roots.
  5. Use the Routh array to find the number of $j\omega$-axis roots of $s^4 + 5s^2 + 4 = 0$.
    Show solution
    The $s^3$ row is all zeros at once. $A(s) = s^4 + 5s^2 + 4$, $dA/ds = 4s^3 + 10s$. Continuing gives no sign changes, so there are no RHP roots, and by symmetry all four roots ($\pm j$, $\pm j2$) lie on the $j\omega$ axis: marginally stable.

    Hint. With positive feedback, the characteristic equation is $1 - G(s)H(s) = 0$.

    Hint

    Hint. Same method as Problem 30: a full zero row gives $K$, and the row above gives $\omega$.
