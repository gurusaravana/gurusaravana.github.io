Part 1. Programming methods

There are three ways to program an industrial robot:

  1. Leadthrough programming. The work cycle is taught by moving the manipulator through the motions while the controller records them for later playback.
    • Powered leadthrough: the programmer drives the joints with buttons or a joystick on a teach pendant. It suits point-to-point robots (spot welding, machine loading).
    • Manual leadthrough: the programmer physically moves the arm, or a light replica of it, along the path. It suits continuous-path robots (spray painting, arc welding), where a smooth human motion is the program.
  2. Textual robot languages. Commands are typed into the controller, as in VAL, RAPID (ABB), KRL (KUKA) or KAREL (FANUC). Points are usually still taught with the pendant, but the language adds program logic, sensor input, control of external equipment, calculations and communication with supervisory computers.
  3. Simulation and off-line programming (OLP). The program is written and tested on a computer model of the cell, then downloaded to the controller, with no need to tie up the robot.

Leadthrough: pros and cons

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Easily learned by shop-floor staffThe robot is out of production while it is being taught
A natural, logical way to teach a motionLittle program logic: few decisions, loops or calculations
No computer programming neededHard to link with sensors and supervisory (cell or plant) control

Whatever the method, programming involves four activities: power-up and preparation, entering and editing the program, compiling it (error checks and post-processing), and running it. Not every robot needs all four as separate steps. Robots are taught through teach pendants, teach terminals or the controller's front panel.

On-line versus off-line

On-line (at the robot)Off-line (away from the robot)
Connectionreal-time link between programmer and manipulatorno physical connection while programming
Downtimethe robot produces nothing while it is taughtcell keeps running; downtime only for final touch-up

One slide labels structured languages "AKA lead-through programming". That label belongs to the third method, manual real-time (on-line) programming. Structured languages are normally used off line, together with simulation.

Simulation of robot cells

A cell simulation is a model of the real system on which we run experiments, then carry the best solution back to the real cell.

Simulation is used for four tasks:

  • Off-line programming: paths, I/O logic and cycle times prepared and debugged before the robot exists or while it works.
  • Evaluation and set-up: what-if studies of robot position and model, part entry and exit, conveyor positions and interference with the plant layout. The whole cell can be "walked through" virtually.
  • Ergonomics and safety: operators can be modelled with the equipment, safety devices tested, and the cell viewed from every angle before it is built. Nobody has to stand in the work envelope while programming.
  • Demonstration: showing customers and management the cell before money is spent.

Commercial packages include ABB RobotStudio, KUKA.Sim and FANUC Roboguide. They use the real controller software in a "virtual robot", so motion and cycle times match the real robot closely. Typical features:

FeatureWhat it does
Cycle-time checktimes the program as the real controller would run it

A generic robot language

The commands below are in the style of VAL, the first widely used robot language. Every commercial language has the same groups of commands under different names.

CommandMeaning
Motion
HERE P1record the present position as point P1 (used during leadthrough)
MOVE P1move to P1 by joint interpolation (fastest, path not straight)
MOVES P1move to P1 along a straight line
DMOVE(4, 125)move joint 4 by an increment of 125 (degrees or mm)
APPROACH P1, 40 MMgo to a point 40 mm from P1 along the tool axis
DEPART 40 MMback away 40 mm along the tool axis
DEFINE PATH123 = PATH(P1, P2, P3) then MOVE PATH123define and follow a path through several points
SPEED 75run later moves at 75 % of full speed
Interlocks and sensors
WAIT 20, ONwait until input 20 is on (for example, "machine finished")
SIGNAL 10, ONswitch output 10 on (for example, "start machine")
SIGNAL 10, 6.0set analogue output 10 to 6.0 V
REACT 25, SAFESTOPwatch input 25 all the time; if it changes, stop safely
Gripper
OPEN, CLOSEopen or close the gripper
CLOSE 25 MMclose to an opening of 25 mm
CLOSE 2.0 Nclose with a gripping force of 2 N

Example: load and unload one machine. The robot takes a blank from P1, loads it at P2, waits for the machine and puts the finished part at P3. Input 20 means "machine done", output 10 starts the machine, and input 25 is the safety-fence door.

REACT 25, SAFESTOP        ; stop at once if the fence door opens
SPEED 75
10 APPROACH P1, 40 MM     ; above the blank
   OPEN
   MOVES P1
   CLOSE 2.0 N
   DEPART 40 MM
   APPROACH P2, 40 MM     ; into the machine
   MOVES P2
   OPEN
   DEPART 40 MM
   SIGNAL 10, ON          ; start the cycle
   WAIT 20, ON            ; wait for "done"
   SIGNAL 10, OFF
   APPROACH P2, 40 MM
   MOVES P2
   CLOSE 2.0 N
   DEPART 40 MM
   MOVE P3                ; to the out conveyor
   OPEN
   GOTO 10

Straight-line moves (MOVES) are used near the part, where the path matters, and joint moves (MOVE) in free space, where speed matters.

Part 2. Industrial applications

Industrial robot applications fall into three groups:

  1. Material handling: material transfer (pick-and-place, palletising) and machine loading and unloading.
  2. Processing operations: spot and arc welding, spray coating, cutting (including plasma arc), grinding and deburring, adhesive application, drilling.
  3. Assembly and inspection.

Why use a robot at all? Robots can work in hazardous or sterile environments, work 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, repeat a task exactly, be more precise than people, and be reprogrammed for a new job. They come in many sizes, are fast, and cost little per hour to run.

Welding

Robots do both spot welding (point-to-point, very common on car bodies) and arc welding (continuous path). Welding suits robots because it is hazardous (fumes, ultraviolet light, sparks), it is an unpleasant job in heavy protective clothing, loads can be heavy, and a robot gives uniform quality.

The main gain is arc-on time, the fraction of the shift during which the arc is actually burning. A manual welder manages only 20–30 %, because of setting up, repositioning, changing rods and resting. A robot in a well-designed cell is close to the production of four welders. Robots can also follow weave patterns (a zigzag across the joint for wide welds) and, with line tracking, weld parts on a moving assembly line.

In the cell above the robot performs flux-cored arc welding (FCAW) at one station while a fitter changes parts at the other, behind a screen. Loading time is hidden inside welding time, so the robot hardly ever waits.

Machine loading and unloading

Robots load presses, machine tools, forging presses and die-casting machines. The reasons are safety (heavy parts, the risk of losing fingers or hands at a press), avoiding slowdowns, high operating speed, small clearances that make hand feeding tricky, and less scrap. The common layouts are:

  1. one robot loading and unloading one machine;
  2. one robot serving several machines doing the same job in parallel;
  3. sequential loading: one robot moves each part from machine to machine;
  4. robots in forging and die-casting.

Worked problem. A robot serves four milling machines. The cell has an 8 s cycle time and makes over 28 000 parts in two 8-hour shifts. Do the four mills work in series (each part visits all four) or in parallel (each mill machines complete parts)?

$$\text{Series: } \frac{1\ \text{part}}{8\ \text{s}} \times 3600\ \tfrac{\text{s}}{\text{h}} \times 16\ \tfrac{\text{h}}{\text{day}} = 7200\ \text{parts/day}$$

$$\text{Parallel: } 7200 \times 4\ \text{machines} = 28\,800\ \text{parts/day}$$

Only the parallel layout reaches 28 000, so the mills work in parallel. In series the line can never be faster than one part per cycle, however many machines it has.

Positioning problems. A polar robot with axis limits may struggle to pick and place at several heights. Remedies are a bigger work envelope, suction-cup pick-up, better rack design, a more flexible end effector, or double grippers that unload the finished part and load the next blank in one visit.

Sequential loading. In the slides' example one robot loads three machines, with an in-conveyor and an out-conveyor, and production rises by about 60 %. Double grippers are of little value here, because only one part is worked at a time and there is no room in the cell for half-finished parts.

Forging and die-casting. Parts are hot, the environment is hot and noisy, and hand feeding risks amputation. A forging robot is programmed to dip its hand in a cooling bath at intervals, and the gripper must cope with the part changing shape as it is forged.

Spray painting

Paint booths are hard to keep within health and safety limits, and people find it hard to paint consistently. A skilled painter teaches the robot once, usually by manual leadthrough, and it repeats the path exactly. Line tracking lets it paint parts on a continuously moving conveyor. Some failures are expected, so a manual touch-up area is often kept, and keeping the robot busy (equipment utilisation) is an important factor in the economics. Adhesive and sealant application is similar.

Fabrication: drilling

Robots drill small fixtured parts and circuit boards, and large parts such as aircraft panels. The drill must be perpendicular to the surface. A person judges this by eye, which is a source of error; a robot computes the surface normal from the part model and holds it exactly.

Assembly and inspection

The usual "3D" reasons for robots (dull, dirty, dangerous) say little about assembly. Assembly needs precision, repeatability, a variety of motions and sophisticated, sometimes compound, grippers. Robots are chosen for it to save labour, to take over a boring repetitive job, to work fast, to be accurate, and to prevent omitted or substituted parts, whether accidental or deliberate.

  • Engine assembly: blocks travel on pallets; auxiliary conveyors bring sub-assemblies and vibratory bowls feed screws and bolts; a magazine of grippers lets one robot do several jobs.
  • Electrical and electronic assembly: indexed pallet carriers, tactile sensing and visual feedback give the "feel" that assembly needs, and also detect defects.
  • General assembly robots that can sense and assemble almost anything of suitable size are needed for flexible manufacturing systems.

Beyond the factory

Robots also work in aerospace and space exploration (the Sojourner Mars rover), underwater and volcanic exploration, medicine (surgery and rehabilitation), the military, service and entertainment, construction, hazardous-material clean-up, and even sheep shearing. Nano-robots are an active research field.

Exercises

  1. For each job, choose powered leadthrough, manual leadthrough or off-line programming, and say why: (a) spot welding 40 points on a car door; (b) spray painting a chair; (c) a new arc-welding cell for a part that is still being designed.
    Show solution
    (a) Powered leadthrough with a teach pendant: point-to-point, and the exact points matter more than the path. (b) Manual leadthrough: continuous path, and the skilled painter's smooth motion is the program. (c) Off-line programming from the CAD model: the robot and part do not exist yet, and simulation checks reach, collisions and cycle time first.
  2. A robot is taught on line for 6 hours per new product, and there are 10 new products a year. The cell earns 4000 birr per hour. Off-line programming would leave 1 hour of touch-up per product. What does it save each year?
    Show solution
    On line: $10 \times 6 = 60$ h of lost production. Off line: $10 \times 1 = 10$ h. Saving: $50 \times 4000 = 200\,000$ birr per year, before the cost of the simulation software and the programmer's time.
  3. Write a short program in the generic language that picks a part from P1 and places it at P2, approaching both points from 50 mm above and closing the gripper to 30 mm. It must not start until input 5 ("part present") is on.
    Show solution
    SPEED 60
WAIT 5, ON
OPEN
APPROACH P1, 50 MM
MOVES P1
CLOSE 30 MM
DEPART 50 MM
APPROACH P2, 50 MM
MOVES P2
OPEN
DEPART 50 MM
  4. A manual welder has an arc-on time of 25 %. A robot in a two-station cell reaches 85 %. If both weld at the same travel speed, how many manual welders does one robot replace? If each welder costs 30 000 birr a month and the robot cell costs 2.4 million birr, roughly how long is the simple payback?
    Show solution
    $85/25 = 3.4$, so about 3 to 4 welders, which matches the "four welders" quoted in the notes. Replacing 3.4 welders saves about $3.4 \times 30\,000 = 102\,000$ birr a month, so payback is about $2\,400\,000/102\,000 \approx 23.5$ months, ignoring running costs (Lesson 3 shows the full payback method).
  5. The four-mill cell in the notes is changed to three shifts of 8 hours, and each mill now takes 10 s per part. What is the daily output if the mills work in parallel? In series?
    Show solution
    Parallel: $(3600/10) \times 24 \times 4 = 34\,560$ parts/day. Series: $(3600/10) \times 24 = 8640$ parts/day.
  6. Why are double grippers useful in a one-machine loading cell but of little value in the sequential cell of the notes?
    Show solution
    In a one-machine cell the robot can unload the finished part and load the next blank in a single visit, so the machine waits less. In the sequential cell each part goes from machine to machine and there is no room for extra half-finished parts, so there is rarely a second part to carry.
  7. Give two reasons why robots are used in (a) die-casting, (b) PCB drilling and (c) assembly of electronic products.
    Show solution
    (a) Hot parts and a hot, noisy workplace; risk of injury at the machine. (b) Speed and accuracy over thousands of holes; the drill must stay exactly perpendicular. (c) Repeatability and precision; vision and touch sensing detect defects and missing parts.
