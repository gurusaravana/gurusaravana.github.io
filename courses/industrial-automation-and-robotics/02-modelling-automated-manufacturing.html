Notes

1. Automated manufacturing systems

Manufacturing is the process of producing a product that meets a specific purpose. An automated manufacturing system (AMS) has computerised control built into the manufacturing equipment. Because so many manufacturing tasks repeat, they suit computer control well.

AMS are used more and more in modern industry: aircraft-part manufacturing, surface treatment of metal parts, printed circuit boards in electroplating plants, wet etching of wafers in semiconductor fabs, food processing and medical laboratories.

In its most developed form an AMS controls every stage of a product, from its design to its dispatch. Most are used for inventory tracking, record keeping, production scheduling and production control.

Common features of an AMS:

  • Shared data. Design data go to the accounting department for a cost–benefit analysis; the design and cost data go to management for approval; the whole set then goes to production so it can tool up.
  • Sensors collect data from the process, most often temperature, pressure, motion, flow and light. The data are turned into information the AMS uses to do its task.
  • CAD and CAM. Computer-aided design (CAD) gives the designer precise drawing tools, standard drawing layouts and rotatable 3D views; CAD uses vector graphics, so each part of the design can be viewed and changed on its own. Computer-aided manufacturing (CAM) turns the CAD design into instructions and data for the computer-controlled machines.
People in an AMSRole
Supervisorsoversee the system and deal with minor problems
Production plannersadjust production to current and new customer needs
Product designersdesign products, usually with CAD
Maintenance engineersrepair the system and keep it running efficiently

Motives for using an AMS: repetitive tasks bore people, who then make mistakes; an AMS responds faster to change, removes people from dangerous and dirty work, makes operating costs predictable, controls material use and waste, gives consistent quality and precision, runs almost continuously (no sick days, holidays or sleep) and lets designs be tested on the computer first. These gains have to be balanced against the social cost, such as lost jobs and the need for new skills.

An AMS must be flexible enough to survive several product life cycles, fault tolerant to keep up production when something fails, and resilient to changes in design, demand and product mix. It is also highly capital intensive, so it must give high quality at low cost to repay the investment in a competitive market. That calls for careful design and operating strategies, and those need models.

2. An AMS is a discrete event dynamic system

The state of an AMS (how many parts wait at each machine, which machines are up, where each pallet is) does not change continuously like a temperature. It changes only at the instants when events happen: a raw part arrives, a finished part leaves, a machine fails, a repair ends. Between events nothing changes. Such a system is a discrete event dynamic system (DEDS), and its behaviour depends on the complex interaction of the timing of these events.

Models of an AMS are therefore either analytical (equations that give the performance directly) or discrete event simulation models (a program that steps from event to event). The main quantitative (performance) models are:

  • discrete event simulation,
  • min–max (max-plus) algebra, for deterministic timed systems,
  • Markov chains,
  • stochastic Petri nets,
  • queues and queueing networks.

3. What performance models are for

Models support decisions at every stage.

Planning and design: the number and type of machines; the number of material-handling devices, buffers, pallets and fixtures; the best layout; tool-storage capacity; evaluating candidate configurations; part-type selection, machine grouping, batching, line balancing and scheduling policies.

Operation: after a failure or breakdown, finding the best routes; predicting the effect of adding or removing resources and parts; finding optimal schedules when a machine fails or the part mix or demand changes suddenly.

Basic design questions: central or local storage? push or pull production? shared or distributed resources? how much flexibility is worth paying for? A faithful model that predicts performance also increases customers' confidence, and gives operators and designers insight into the system.

4. Performance measures

MeasureMeaning
Throughput (production rate)finished parts per unit time
Manufacturing lead timetime a part spends in the system, from arrival to departure
Work-in-process (WIP)number of parts in the system at a time
Utilisationfraction of time a machine or robot is busy
Queue length and waiting timehow many parts wait at a resource, and for how long
Due-date performancefraction of orders delivered on time

Two relations hold for almost any stable system:

$$\text{utilisation } \rho = \frac{\lambda}{\mu}, \qquad \text{Little's law: } L = \lambda W$$

where $\lambda$ is the arrival (= throughput) rate, $\mu$ the service rate of the machine, $L$ the average number of parts in the system and $W$ the average time each part spends there. For a single machine with random (Poisson) arrivals and random (exponential) service times, the M/M/1 queue, theory gives $L = \rho/(1-\rho)$.

Worked example. Parts arrive at 0.8 per minute at a machine that can do 1 per minute. Utilisation $\rho = 0.8$. M/M/1 gives $L = 0.8/0.2 = 4$ parts in the system on average, and Little's law gives $W = L/\lambda = 4/0.8 = 5$ minutes per part, although the machine needs only 1 minute. Waiting, not machining, dominates the lead time. At $\rho = 0.9$, $L$ jumps to 9: a busy machine builds long queues.

5. Tools: measurement, simulation and analytical models

Performance measurement is done on an existing system: monitoring key variables, diagnosing failures, deciding how to reconfigure. Performance modelling works before the system exists or alongside it, by simulation or by analytical models.

Discrete event simulation is used in nearly every factory for analysis and decisions. It can model the system in great detail, and with enough runs its estimates are very accurate. But it is computationally expensive, and a detailed model (all part mixes, machine states, routings, flow control and sequencing rules) takes a long time to write.

A simulation study has these steps: build the model; code it as a program; validate the model and debug the program; run it for many random inputs; analyse the outputs statistically to get the performance measures.

Simulation languageNotes
SIMSCRIPTfor decision-support models: military and C4ISR systems, communication networks, transport and manufacturing, inventory and logistics, financial markets
GPSS (General-Purpose Simulation System)process-oriented; the model is a block diagram of standard blocks that GPSS interprets. Easy, but interpretation is slow, so not for very large models
SLAM II (Simulation Language for Alternative Modelling)the first to combine three world views: process-oriented networks, discrete events and continuous variables, in one model
GASP, GASP II, GASP IV (General Activity Simulation Program)FORTRAN-based simulation languages

Analytical models (Markov chains, queues, queueing networks, Petri nets) give results in seconds and show how the performance depends on each parameter, but they need simplifying assumptions. Packages written for manufacturing include CAN-Q, MVAQ and MANUPLAN. A common practice is to screen many configurations quickly with an analytical model, then check the best few in detail by simulation.

Exercises

  1. A robot cell receives on average 12 parts per hour and each part stays in the cell 25 minutes on average. How many parts are in the cell on average?
    Show solution
    Little's law: $L = \lambda W = (12/60\ \text{per min}) \times 25\ \text{min} = 5$ parts.
  2. A machine with mean processing time 4 min gets parts at random, on average one every 5 min. Treat it as M/M/1. Find the utilisation, the average number of parts at the machine and the average time a part spends there.
    Show solution
    $\mu = 0.25$/min, $\lambda = 0.2$/min, $\rho = 0.8$. $L = 0.8/0.2 = 4$ parts. $W = L/\lambda = 20$ min, of which 4 min is processing and 16 min is waiting.
  3. List the events and the state variables you would use to simulate a line of two machines with a buffer between them, where machine 2 sometimes breaks down.
    Show solution
    State: parts in the buffer, status of each machine (idle, busy, blocked, down). Events: part arrival at machine 1, end of processing at machine 1 (part moves to buffer, or machine 1 is blocked if the buffer is full), start and end of processing at machine 2, failure of machine 2, end of repair of machine 2, departure of a finished part.
  4. In the simulation above, set λ = 0.8, μ = 1, K = 20 and the repair time to 0. Press "New random run" five times and note the average number in the system. Why do the runs differ, and how does the average compare with the M/M/1 value of 4?
    Show solution
    Each run uses different random arrival and service times, so a single 8-hour run gives only an estimate. The values scatter around 4 (some well above, some below). That is why simulation studies use many runs, or long runs, and report averages with confidence intervals.
  5. Give one decision each that a performance model helps with (a) when designing an AMS, (b) while running it.
    Show solution
    (a) For example how many machines, pallets or buffer spaces to buy, or which layout to use. (b) For example how to reroute parts and reschedule when a machine fails, or what happens if the product mix changes.
  6. When would you choose an analytical queueing model rather than discrete event simulation?
    Show solution
    Early in design, when many alternatives must be compared quickly and approximate answers are enough, or when you need to see how the result depends on each parameter. Use simulation when details (routing rules, failures, blocking, non-random timing) matter and you need accurate numbers for a few final candidates.
