Notes

1. What is automation?

Automation is the use of control systems and information technology to reduce the human work needed to produce goods and services. Machinery, factory processes, boilers, heat-treating ovens, telephone switching and the steering and stabilisation of ships and aircraft are all run this way with little or no human intervention.

For manufacturing, a more precise definition is: all measures that make a process run wholly or partly according to a previously set program, without a human having to control it. A classic phrase sums up where automation sits: automation begins where mass production leaves off. It is mass production taken to the point where human labour and human control are replaced, completely or nearly completely, by mechanical labour and mechanical (today: computer) control.

A truly automated system has three abilities:

  1. the ability to make a decision,
  2. the ability to carry out that decision,
  3. the ability to evaluate and correct its own performance (feedback).

Manual assembly has all three, but in the worker's head and hands. Mechanization gives the machine the second ability only; adding control technology gives it the first and third.

Good automation gives the left column to the machine and keeps people for the right column: supervision, problem solving and improvement.

The technology of automation includes automatic machine tools, automatic assembly machines, industrial robots, automatic material handling and storage, automatic inspection and quality control, feedback and computer process control, and computer systems for planning, data collection and decision making.

Each field brings its own demands. Oil and gas plants work in explosive atmospheres; the upstream side (from the well to the refinery, often under the sea) adds high pressure, salt water and poor access. Process industries such as steel, cement, glass and paper run a continuous flow of material, often 24 hours a day. Power plants must control fuel supply and the primary process, protect people and the neighbourhood, watch the environmental impact, hold voltage and frequency and be available 24/365.

4. The automation hierarchy

A large plant is not run by one controller. Control is split into levels, each with its own job, time scale and amount of data.

LevelWhat it does
5 · Administrationfinance, human resources, documentation, long-term planning
4 · Enterprise / manufacturingsets production goals, plans resources, coordinates sites, manages orders; manages execution, workflow, quality supervision, scheduling and maintenance (MES)
3 · Supervisorysupervises production and the site, optimises, shows the plant to operators, stores process data, logs operations and history (open loop)
2 · Group (area)controls a well-defined part of the plant: coordinates subgroups, adjusts set-points and parameters, commands several units as a whole (closed loop, except when an operator steps in)
1 · Unit (cell)controls part of a group: measure (sampling, scaling, calibration), control (regulation, set-points) and command (sequencing, protection, interlocking)
0 · Fieldsensors and actuators: data acquisition and transmission, no processing beyond measurement correction and built-in protection

The field level is in direct contact with the plant hardware (the "primary technology"). The group level coordinates several unit controllers; it is often hierarchical, but group controllers can also talk peer to peer, which is the idea of a distributed control system (DCS). A group can have its own local operator panel, for example a cement-packaging line with a console and an emergency panel for maintenance.

At the supervisory level the operator works through a human–machine interface. In the 1970s every instrument was wired directly to a mimic wall in the control room. Today a SCADA system (Supervisory Control And Data Acquisition) and large projection screens replace the mimic wall, and nothing is wired directly to the plant any more. SCADA:

  • shows the current state of the process (visualisation),
  • shows alarms and events (alarm log, logbook),
  • shows and analyses trends from the historian,
  • gives access to handbooks, data sheets, inventory and expert systems,
  • exchanges data with other control centres.

Above it, plant management keeps plant and product data safely so that processes can be tracked and products traced (a Plant Information Management System, PIMS) and predicts future behaviour, especially equipment maintenance, while tracking key performance indicators (asset optimisation). The engineering workplace manages the control system itself rather than the plant: it configures networks and devices, loads software, sets access rights and troubleshoots.

Data and the hierarchy. The lowest levels need the fastest response and handle the most raw data, but the processing is simple (it was once done in hardware). Going up, data are condensed into summary information, timing becomes relaxed, the processing becomes more complex (it needs models), and history is stored. The SCADA level presents complex data to the operator and helps with decisions and maintenance, which needs a knowledge base besides the plant database.

Why data reduction matters: a plant sends more than 5 kbit/s of information towards its staff, but a person can process only about 25 bit/s. A power plant once needed some 200 engineers to watch it; with computers doing the reduction, about three are enough. A modern nuclear plant has around 10 000 points (8 000 measurements and 2 000 actuators) and about 1 000 controllers; an electricity distribution network has roughly three times more points than a conventional power plant.

5. Fixed, programmable and flexible automation

Fixed automation (also called hard automation): the sequence of operations is fixed by the equipment itself. It suits very high quantities, needs a high initial investment in custom-built equipment, gives high production rates and copes poorly with product variety. Examples: automated assembly machines, chemical processes, conveyor systems, machining transfer lines, paint and coating lines, web handling. A lamp factory turning out more than a billion bulbs a year is the classic case.

Programmable automation: the sequence can be changed by reprogramming, so different product configurations can be made. It needs a high investment in general-purpose programmable equipment (CNC machines, robots), gives lower production rates than fixed automation, and suits batch production. Between batches the physical set-up (tools, fixtures) and the part program must be changed, which costs time.

Flexible automation: an extension of programmable automation that changes over from one job to the next with almost no lost time, so a mix of different products can flow continuously. It needs a high investment in a custom-engineered system and gives medium production rates. One motorcycle maker is reported to have launched more than a hundred models in 18 months on flexible lines.

TypeWhen to considerAdvantagesDisadvantages
Fixedhigh demand, long product lifemaximum efficiency, low unit costlarge investment, inflexible

The automated flow line, sometimes called "Detroit-type" automation after the car engine lines where it began, is the typical fixed system. Its objectives are to reduce labour cost, raise production rates, reduce work-in-process, shorten the distances parts travel and integrate operations.

6. Why automate, and how to go about it

Reasons for automating: (1) raise labour productivity, (2) reduce labour cost, (3) ease labour shortages, (4) remove routine manual and clerical tasks, (5) improve worker safety, (6) improve product quality, (7) reduce manufacturing lead time, (8) do what cannot be done by hand, (9) avoid the high cost of not automating (losing to competitors).

Automation is not always the right answer. Three approaches help decide.

The USA principle is a common-sense recipe:

  1. Understand the existing process: what are the inputs and outputs, what happens between them, what is the function of the process, how does it add value, what is the sequence of operations? (Tools: input/output analysis, value-chain analysis, charting, mathematical models.)
  2. Simplify it: what is the purpose of each step and each move? Is it necessary? Can it be removed, combined with another step or done with better technology?
  3. Automate what is left, using the ten strategies and the migration strategy below.

Ten strategies for automation and process improvement:

#StrategyIdea
1Specialisation of operationsspecial-purpose equipment for one operation
2Combined operationsseveral operations at one machine: fewer workstations
3Simultaneous operationsdo the combined operations at the same time: less processing time
4Integration of operationslink workstations with automatic handling
5Increased flexibilitymake the most of the equipment across products
6Better material handling and storagecut non-productive waiting and moving time
7On-line inspectioncheck during the process and correct at once
8Process control and optimisationrun each process and machine more efficiently
9Plant operations controlcontrol at the level of the whole plant
10Computer-integrated manufacturing (CIM)link design, planning and the factory through computers

Automation migration strategy for a new product: Phase 1 manual production in single-station manned cells (quick to start, low risk); Phase 2 automated single-station cells working independently, once demand is proven; Phase 3 an integrated multi-station automated system with automated material handling, once demand is large and stable.

7. Industries, production systems and types of production

There are two kinds of industry. Manufacturing industries make discrete items such as cars, computers and machine tools and their parts. Process industries make chemicals, plastics, petroleum products, food, soap, steel and cement. Companies can also be grouped by what they do with material:

  • basic producers turn natural resources into raw materials (iron ore into steel ingots),
  • converters turn raw materials into industrial products (ingots into bar stock and sheet; petroleum into plastics),
  • fabricators make and assemble final products (bar stock into engine parts, sheet into car body panels).

Manufacturing turns raw materials into goods using machines, on a large scale; the output is always tangible. Production is wider: any transformation of material and non-material inputs (ideas, skills, information) into something useful, goods or services, with or without machines. All manufacturing is production, but not all production is manufacturing.

A production system is the people, equipment and procedures that do the manufacturing. It has two parts: the facilities (factory, equipment, layout) and the manufacturing support systems (the procedures used to manage production). Automation can be applied to both: automation of the manufacturing systems and computerisation of the support systems. Where the two overlap through computers we speak of computer-integrated manufacturing (CIM).

Type of productionVolume and varietyEquipment and peopleExamples
Job shoplow volume, often one of a kind, great variety, to customer ordergeneral-purpose, flexible equipment; highly skilled workersaircraft, space vehicles, machine tools, special tools, prototypes
Batchmedium-sized lots, made once or at intervals; the plant can produce faster than demand, so it builds stock and then changes overgeneral-purpose equipment with set-ups between batchesindustrial equipment, furniture, textbooks, parts for household appliances
Masscontinuous production of identical products at very high rates and demandspecial-purpose equipment dedicated to the product; high investmentquantity production of single parts (presses, moulding machines); flow production in refineries, chemical and food plants and engine or car assembly lines

Functions in manufacturing. Whatever the type of production, five functions turn raw material into finished product:

  1. Processing adds energy (mechanical, heat, electrical, chemical) to change the part, without adding components. Four kinds: basic processes give the initial shape (casting, moulding); secondary processes give the final geometry (turning, drilling, milling, blanking, forming, drawing); property-enhancing operations improve the material, not its shape (heat treatment, pre-shrinking cloth); finishing operations improve appearance or protect (polishing, painting, chrome plating). A process needs five inputs (raw material, equipment, tooling and fixtures, energy, labour) and gives two outputs (the finished part, and scrap and waste).
  2. Assembly joins two or more components: mechanical fastening (screws, nuts, rivets) and joining (welding, brazing, soldering).
  3. Material handling and storage: parts spend far more time being moved and stored than being processed, and much of the cost of production goes here.
  4. Inspection and test: checks that the product meets its design standards and specifications.
  5. Control: regulates each processing and assembly operation to meet its targets, and manages the plant as a whole (labour, maintenance, moving materials).

8. Information processing in manufacturing

A company may make hundreds of products, each with thousands of components. Coordinating everything needed to make the parts, assemble them and deliver the product is a problem in information processing. Four functions form a cycle:

  • Business functions talk to the customer and are the start and end of the cycle: sales, marketing, forecasting, order entry, cost accounting and billing.
  • Product design turns customer specifications or a new idea from sales into a design: research and development, design engineering, drafting and prototypes.
  • Manufacturing planning decides how to make it: manufacturing engineering, industrial engineering, production planning and control.
  • Manufacturing control runs the physical operations to carry out the plan: shop-floor control, quality control and inventory control.

9. Automated flow lines

An automated flow line (AFL) is a set of workstations linked by work-handling devices that transfer parts from station to station. Transfer and processing are both automatic. Manual stations can be included for operations that are hard or uneconomical to automate. The objectives are to reduce labour cost, raise production rates, reduce work-in-process, minimise distances between operations, and achieve specialisation and integration of operations.

  • In-line: stations in a more or less straight line.
  • Segmented in-line: two or more straight sections, usually at right angles (L, U or rectangular shapes).
  • Rotary (dial indexing machine): parts ride on a round table that indexes them to fixed stations around its edge.

Methods of work-part transport. The transfer system must not only move the parts but also orient and locate them correctly at each station.

  • Continuous transfer: parts move at constant speed, so the workheads must move with them while they work. This is impractical for heavy machining heads (inertia), but ideal for bottling, packaging, manual assembly where the worker walks with the line, and simple automatic assembly. In a bottling machine the bottles ride round a rotating drum while spouts fill them; the liquid moves steadily, so there is no inertia problem.
  • Intermittent (synchronous) transfer: all parts move together, stop, are registered at their stations, worked on, then move again.
  • Asynchronous (power-and-free) transfer: each part moves on as soon as its own station is finished, independently of the others. Some parts are being worked while others are travelling.

The best choice depends on the operations, the number of stations, the size and weight of the parts, whether there are manual stations, the production rate required and how well the station times are balanced.

Transfer mechanisms. Linear mechanisms for in-line machines:

  • Walking beam: a transfer bar lifts the parts from their stations, moves them one station forward and lowers them into nests that locate them accurately. The beam motion usually comes from a motor-driven camshaft or a hydraulic cylinder.
  • Powered roller conveyor: rollers driven by a flat belt underneath (friction) or by a chain carry parts or pallets with flat bottoms. Easy to divert pallets into stations or side tracks.
  • Chain-drive conveyor: a chain or flexible steel belt pulls work carriers, running "over and under" (pulleys on horizontal axes) or "around the corner" (pulleys on vertical axes). It can give continuous, intermittent or asynchronous motion; in asynchronous use the parts are dragged by friction or float on an oil film, and need a final locating device at each station.

Rotary mechanisms index a dial table through equal angles:

  • Rack and pinion: a piston drives a rack that turns a pinion on the table; a clutch gives one direction. Simple but not suited to high speed.
  • Ratchet and pawl: a toothed wheel and a spring-loaded pawl allow motion one way only; the pawl clicks over the teeth one way and locks against them the other.
  • Geneva mechanism: a continuously turning driver indexes the table (below).
  • Cam mechanisms: a cam can be shaped to give almost any velocity and dwell profile.

With $n$ slots the wheel turns $360°/n$ per revolution of the driver. The pin enters and leaves each slot along the slot's centre line, which gives

$$\text{index angle of the driver} = 180° - \frac{360°}{n}, \qquad \text{dwell angle} = 180° + \frac{360°}{n}$$

For $n = 6$: index $120°$, dwell $240°$. For $n = 4$: index $90°$, dwell $270°$. Four, five, six and eight slots are the usual choices. The distance between the shafts $c$ fixes the crank radius $r = c\sin(180°/n)$ and the wheel radius $R = c\cos(180°/n)$.

The slide on cam mechanisms says that "Geneva mechanisms provide probably the most accurate and reliable method of indexing the dial". In the source text (Groover) this sentence is about cam mechanisms: they are the most accurate and reliable indexers and are widely used even though they cost more than the alternatives.

10. Control functions of an automated flow line

A flow line carries out a huge number of steps in sequence, so its control has three main functions:

  1. Sequence control coordinates the actions of the transfer system and the stations with split-second timing.
  2. Safety monitoring makes sure the line never runs in an unsafe state: for example sensors check that a cutting tool is still in good condition before the next part is machined, and that critical steps were all done, in the right order.
  3. Quality monitoring checks quality attributes of the parts so that defective parts and assemblies are found and possibly rejected, either inside existing stations or at separate inspection stations.

When a fault is found, the controller can react in two ways. Instantaneous control stops the line at once and finds the cause, which protects the line but loses production. Memory control lets the faulty part continue but remembers it: later stations skip it, and it is rejected at the end, so the line keeps running.

Exercises

  1. A dial table is indexed by an eight-slot Geneva mechanism whose driver turns at 30 rpm. Find the index and dwell angles of the driver, and the index and dwell times per cycle.
    Show solution
    Index angle $= 180° - 360°/8 = 135°$; dwell $= 225°$. One driver revolution takes $60/30 = 2$ s, so index time $= 2 \times 135/360 = 0.75$ s and dwell time $= 1.25$ s. The stations have 1.25 s to work on each part.
  2. Classify each as fixed, programmable or flexible automation: (a) a soft-drink bottling line; (b) a CNC machining centre that makes 50 brackets, then 30 flanges after a tool and program change; (c) a welding line that builds three car models in any order without stopping.
    Show solution
    (a) Fixed: one product, very high volume, sequence built into the machine. (b) Programmable: batch production with set-up between batches. (c) Flexible: a mix of products with no lost changeover time.
  3. In a cement plant, put each item on its hierarchy level: a thermocouple in the kiln; the PID loop that holds kiln temperature; the coordination of the kiln, cooler and mill sections; the operator alarm screen; order tracking and the shift production schedule; the yearly budget.
    Show solution
    Thermocouple: level 0 (field). PID loop: level 1 (unit). Coordinating the kiln, cooler and mill: level 2 (group). Alarm screen: level 3 (supervisory, SCADA). Order tracking and shift schedule: level 4 (MES). Yearly budget: level 5 (administration and enterprise, ERP).
  4. A plant has 10 000 measurement points, each sampled once a second as a 16-bit value. Compare the raw data rate with the 25 bit/s a person can take in. What does this tell you about the supervisory level?
    Show solution
    Raw rate $= 10\,000 \times 16 = 160\,000$ bit/s, which is $6400$ times what a person can absorb. The supervisory level must condense the data (alarms, trends, summaries, exception reports) before showing it to the operator.
  5. For each product, name the most likely type of production: (a) a prototype satellite; (b) school textbooks reprinted every year; (c) motor oil; (d) M8 bolts by the million.
    Show solution
    (a) Job shop (one of a kind). (b) Batch (medium lots repeated at intervals). (c) Mass, flow production (continuous process). (d) Mass, quantity production of a single part on standard machines.
  6. Apply the USA principle to manual drilling of four holes in a plate: the worker marks the holes, centre-punches, drills them one at a time on a pillar drill and then deburrs. Suggest a simplification before automating.
    Show solution
    Understand: input plate, output plate with four deburred holes; marking and punching only locate the holes. Simplify: a drill jig removes marking and punching; a multi-spindle head drills all four at once (combined and simultaneous operations); a drill with a chamfer step removes the separate deburring. Only then automate the simplified process, for example a pneumatic clamp and a power feed on the multi-spindle head.
