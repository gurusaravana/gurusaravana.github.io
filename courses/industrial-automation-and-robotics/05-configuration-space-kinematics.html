Notes

Lesson 4 described a robot by its Denavit–Hartenberg table. This lesson steps back and asks the most basic question, where is the robot? Part A answers it with the configuration space and a formula that counts the degrees of freedom of any mechanism. Part B answers it with forward kinematics: the position and orientation of the tool as a function of the joint values.

Part A. Configuration and degrees of freedom

The configuration of a robot is a complete specification of the position of every point of the robot. Because the links are rigid and of known shape, a few numbers are enough. The smallest number $n$ of real-valued coordinates needed is the robot's degrees of freedom (dof), and the $n$-dimensional space of all configurations is the configuration space (C-space).

A coin that can lie heads or tails up still has 3 dof: the side that is up takes only two discrete values, not a continuous range.

Counting freedoms of a rigid body

Pick three points $A$, $B$, $C$ on the coin. Placed freely in the plane they would have 6 coordinates, but the body is rigid, so the three distances $d_{AB}$, $d_{BC}$, $d_{AC}$ cannot change:

$$(x_A - x_B)^2 + (y_A - y_B)^2 = d_{AB}^2 \quad\text{and similarly for } BC,\ AC.$$

$A$ can go anywhere (2 freedoms). $B$ must lie on a circle round $A$ (2 − 1 = 1 freedom, the angle $\phi_{AB}$). $C$ is then fixed by two distance constraints (2 − 2 = 0). So the coin has 3 dof, described by $(x_A, y_A, \phi_{AB})$. A fourth point $D$ adds three distance equations but only two of them are independent, so it adds nothing. The general rule is

dof = (sum of freedoms of the points) − (number of independent constraints) = (number of variables) − (number of independent equations).

The same count in space: $A$ has 3 freedoms, $B$ lies on a sphere round $A$ (2), and $C$ lies on the circle where two spheres meet (1). A spatial rigid body has 6 dof (three linear, three angular) and a planar rigid body has 3 (two linear, one angular).

The rigid-body constraint in equation 2.12 of the slides is printed as $(x_A - y_A)^2 + (x_B - y_B)^2 = d_{AB}$, which mixes the coordinates. It should read $(x_A - x_B)^2 + (y_A - y_B)^2 = d_{AB}^2$, as above.

Grübler's formula

Take a mechanism with $N$ links, counting the ground as a link, and $J$ joints, where joint $i$ has $f_i$ freedoms. Each moving link has $m$ freedoms ($m = 3$ in the plane, $6$ in space) and each joint removes $m - f_i$ of them:

$$\text{dof} = m(N - 1) - \sum_{i=1}^{J}(m - f_i) = m(N - 1 - J) + \sum_{i=1}^{J} f_i$$

Where three links meet at one pin, count two revolute joints, not one, because each joint connects exactly two links. Forgetting this is the most common Grübler mistake.

The slides' "planar mechanism with overlapping joints" example gives counts that do not agree with each other ($N = 8$ in one line and $N = 7$ in the next, with the same $J$). It is left out here; use the rule in the box above instead.

The 12 dof of the SPS version looks surprising, but it is right: each leg can now spin about its own axis. That spin does not move the platform, so the platform itself still has 6 dof.

When Grübler's formula fails. The formula only counts; it assumes the constraints are independent. The parallelogram linkage ($N = 5$, $J = 6$) gives $3(5-1-6) + 6 = 0$, a rigid structure, yet if the three parallel links have equal length it moves with 1 dof, because one constraint is redundant. A five-bar linkage with its two ground joints locked should be rigid, but if the two middle links overlap they can still swing together. Prototypes of the 3 × UPU platform also show extra freedoms. These are singularities of closed chains, and Grübler's formula cannot see them.

Describing the configuration space

An explicit parametrisation lists independent coordinates, such as $(x, y, \theta)$ for a planar body. An implicit one lists more coordinates plus the equations that tie them together, such as the four coordinates of two points plus the distance equation above.

For an open chain the joint values are an explicit parametrisation. For the planar 2R arm each angle runs from 0 to $2\pi$ and wraps round, so the C-space is $S^1 \times S^1$, a torus. Cut the torus along two circles and it opens into a square whose opposite edges are glued: the "flat torus".

For a closed chain an implicit description is easier. Treat the four-bar linkage as a 4R serial chain whose tip must come back to the base, pointing along the ground link. This gives three loop-closure equations in four angles:

$$\begin{aligned} L_1\cos\theta_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) + L_3\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2+\theta_3) + L_4\cos(\theta_1+\dots+\theta_4) &= 0 \\ L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) + L_3\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2+\theta_3) + L_4\sin(\theta_1+\dots+\theta_4) &= 0 \\ \theta_1+\theta_2+\theta_3+\theta_4 + \pi &= 0 \end{aligned}$$

Four unknowns and three independent equations leave a curve in the four-dimensional joint space: 1 dof, as Grübler said. In general $k$ independent loop-closure equations $g(\theta) = 0$ in $n$ joint variables (called holonomic constraints) leave a C-space of dimension $n - k$.

Part B. Forward kinematics

Forward kinematics computes the position and orientation of the end-effector frame from the joint values. For the planar 3R arm, plain trigonometry is enough:

$$x = L_1c_1 + L_2c_{12} + L_3c_{123}, \qquad y = L_1s_1 + L_2s_{12} + L_3s_{123}, \qquad \phi = \theta_1+\theta_2+\theta_3$$

where $c_{12} = \cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)$ and so on. If only the tool position matters, the task space is the $x$–$y$ plane and the first two equations are the forward kinematics.

Worked example. $L_1 = 1$, $L_2 = 0.8$, $L_3 = 0.5$ m with $\theta = (30°, 45°, -30°)$: the cumulative angles are 30°, 75° and 45°, so $x = 0.866 + 0.207 + 0.354 = 1.427$ m, $y = 0.500 + 0.773 + 0.354 = 1.626$ m and $\phi = 45°$.

Method 1: link frames

For general spatial chains, trigonometry gets messy. Attach a frame to each link and multiply the transforms between neighbours. For the 3R arm, using $3\times3$ planar ($SE(2)$) matrices:

$$T_{04} = T_{01}T_{12}T_{23}T_{34}, \quad T_{01} = \begin{bmatrix} c_1 & -s_1 & 0 \\ s_1 & c_1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix},\ T_{12} = \begin{bmatrix} c_2 & -s_2 & L_1 \\ s_2 & c_2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix},\ T_{23} = \begin{bmatrix} c_3 & -s_3 & L_2 \\ s_3 & c_3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix},\ T_{34} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & L_3 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$$

$T_{34}$ is constant; every other $T_{i-1,i}$ depends only on its own joint angle.

Method 2: product of exponentials

Instead of link frames, treat each joint axis as a screw axis. Let $M$ be the tool pose with all joints at zero (arm stretched along $x$): $M$ has identity rotation and position $(L_1+L_2+L_3, 0)$. Turning joint 3 alone moves the tool by a screw motion about axis 3, then joint 2 moves links 2 and 3 together, then joint 1 moves everything:

$$T_{04} = e^{[S_1]\theta_1}\,e^{[S_2]\theta_2}\,e^{[S_3]\theta_3}\,M, \qquad [S_i] = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 & y_i \\ 1 & 0 & -x_i \\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$$

where $(x_i, y_i)$ is a point on joint axis $i$ in the zero pose: $(0, 0)$, $(L_1, 0)$ and $(L_1+L_2, 0)$, so the third columns of $[S_2]$ and $[S_3]$ are $(0, -L_1, 0)$ and $(0, -(L_1+L_2), 0)$. This is the product of exponentials (PoE) formula. It needs no link frames, only the base frame, the tool frame and the joint axes.

Method 3: modified Denavit–Hartenberg

Chapter 5B uses the modified (Craig) form of D-H, not the standard form of Lesson 4. Frame $\{i\}$ sits on joint axis $i$, with $\hat z_i$ along the axis, and the four parameters describe how to get from $\{i-1\}$ to $\{i\}$:

  • Link length $a_{i-1}$: the length of the common perpendicular from axis $i-1$ to axis $i$ (not necessarily the length of the physical link).
  • Link twist $\alpha_{i-1}$: the angle from $\hat z_{i-1}$ to $\hat z_i$, measured about $\hat x_{i-1}$.
  • Link offset $d_i$: the distance along $\hat z_i$ from where $\hat x_{i-1}$ meets it to the origin of $\{i\}$.
  • Joint angle $\theta_i$: the angle from $\hat x_{i-1}$ to $\hat x_i$, measured about $\hat z_i$.

$$T_{i-1,i} = \text{Rot}(\hat x, \alpha_{i-1})\,\text{Trans}(\hat x, a_{i-1})\,\text{Trans}(\hat z, d_i)\,\text{Rot}(\hat z, \theta_i) = \begin{bmatrix} c\theta_i & -s\theta_i & 0 & a_{i-1} \\ s\theta_i\, c\alpha_{i-1} & c\theta_i\, c\alpha_{i-1} & -s\alpha_{i-1} & -d_i\, s\alpha_{i-1} \\ s\theta_i\, s\alpha_{i-1} & c\theta_i\, s\alpha_{i-1} & c\alpha_{i-1} & d_i\, c\alpha_{i-1} \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Two of the elementary matrices in the slides have sign or index slips. In $\text{Rot}(\hat x, \alpha)$ (eq. 5.13) the entry in row 3, column 2 is $+\sin\alpha$, not $-\sin\alpha$. In $\text{Rot}(\hat z, \theta_i)$ (eq. 5.16) the angle is $\theta_i$, not $\theta_{i-1}$, and the entry in row 2, column 1 is $+\sin\theta_i$. With those two fixes the product above is correct.

Special cases. If two neighbouring axes intersect, set $a_{i-1} = 0$ and point $\hat x_{i-1}$ perpendicular to the plane of the two axes (either sign is fine). If they are parallel, many common perpendiculars exist: pick the one that makes the most parameters zero. For a prismatic joint, $\hat z$ points along the sliding direction and $d_i$ becomes the joint variable. Choose $\{0\}$ to coincide with $\{1\}$ at the zero position, so $a_0 = \alpha_0 = 0$ and $d_1 = 0$ (or $\theta_1 = 0$ for a prismatic first joint).

Why only four parameters? A general frame-to-frame displacement needs six numbers, but the D-H rules only allow rotations and translations along $\hat x$ and $\hat z$. That is why some frame pairs, for example a pure sideways shift along $\hat y$, cannot be written as D-H parameters, and why a table must always come with a sketch of the frames.

Exercises

  1. How many degrees of freedom does each have: (a) a sliding door; (b) a chess piece on the board, allowing it to turn; (c) an aircraft in flight; (d) a train on its track?
    Show solution
    (a) 1, (b) 3 ($x$, $y$ and the turn), (c) 6, (d) 1 (its distance along the track).
  2. Use "freedoms minus independent constraints" to show that two planar bodies joined by a revolute joint have 4 dof.
    Show solution
    Four points give 8 coordinates. Each body has one distance constraint (2), and the revolute joint makes two points coincide, $x_B = x_C$ and $y_B = y_C$ (2 more). $8 - 4 = 4$, described by $(x_A, y_A, \phi_{AB}, \theta)$.
  3. Find the dof of a planar mechanism with 7 links (including ground) joined by 8 revolute joints and 1 prismatic joint.
    Show solution
    $3(7 - 1 - 9) + 9 = -9 + 9 = 0$. It is a structure (unless some constraint is redundant). Check: if instead it had $N = 8$ links with the same 9 joints, $3(8-1-9)+9 = 3$.
  4. A delta (parallel) robot has a base, a moving platform and three legs. Each leg is a revolute joint at the base, an upper arm, and a parallelogram forearm made of two rods with four spherical joints. Ignoring the spin of each rod about its own axis, count the dof. (Hint: count each parallelogram as 2 rods.)
    Show solution
    Links: base, platform, 3 upper arms, 6 rods: $N = 11$. Joints: 3 R + 12 S: $J = 15$, $\sum f = 3 + 36 = 39$. Grübler: $6(11 - 1 - 15) + 39 = 9$. Each rod can spin about its own axis (6 spins), so the useful dof is $9 - 6 = 3$: the platform translates in $x$, $y$, $z$.
  5. What is the configuration space of (a) a 3R planar arm with no joint limits, (b) a PR arm (prismatic then revolute) in the plane, (c) a 2R arm whose first joint can only move from 0 to 180°?
    Show solution
    (a) $S^1 \times S^1 \times S^1$, a 3-torus. (b) $\mathbb{R} \times S^1$ (or an interval times $S^1$), a cylinder. (c) An interval times $S^1$: a band, not a torus, because the first angle no longer wraps round.
  6. For the planar 3R arm with $L_1 = L_2 = 1$ m, $L_3 = 0.2$ m, find the tool pose for $\theta = (90°, -90°, 0°)$ and for $\theta = (0°, 90°, 90°)$.
    Show solution
    First: cumulative angles 90°, 0°, 0°: $x = 0 + 1 + 0.2 = 1.2$ m, $y = 1 + 0 + 0 = 1$ m, $\phi = 0$. Second: cumulative 0°, 90°, 180°: $x = 1 + 0 - 0.2 = 0.8$ m, $y = 0 + 1 + 0 = 1$ m, $\phi = 180°$.
  7. Write $[S_1]$, $[S_2]$, $[S_3]$ and $M$ for the planar 3R arm and use them to check that the PoE formula gives the right tool position when $\theta_1 = \theta_2 = 0$ and $\theta_3 = 90°$.
    Show solution
    $[S_1]$ has third column $(0, 0, 0)$, $[S_2]$ has $(0, -L_1, 0)$, $[S_3]$ has $(0, -(L_1+L_2), 0)$; all have $\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$ top-left. With only $\theta_3 = 90°$, the tool rotates 90° about the point $(L_1+L_2, 0)$, so it moves from $(L_1+L_2+L_3, 0)$ to $(L_1+L_2, L_3)$, pointing along $+y$. This matches $x = L_1 + L_2 + L_3\cos 90°$, $y = L_3\sin 90°$.
  8. Using the modified D-H matrix, write $T_{01}$ and $T_{12}$ for the first two rows of the 3R spatial chain table.
    Show solution
    Row 1 ($\alpha_0 = 0$, $a_0 = 0$, $d_1 = 0$): $T_{01} = \text{Rot}(\hat z, \theta_1)$. Row 2 ($\alpha_1 = 90°$, $a_1 = L_1$, $d_2 = 0$, angle $\theta_2 - 90°$): with $c\alpha = 0$, $s\alpha = 1$, $T_{12} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\psi & -\sin\psi & 0 & L_1 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 & 0 \\ \sin\psi & \cos\psi & 0 & 0 \\ 0&0&0&1\end{bmatrix}$, $\psi = \theta_2 - 90°$, so $\cos\psi = \sin\theta_2$ and $\sin\psi = -\cos\theta_2$.
