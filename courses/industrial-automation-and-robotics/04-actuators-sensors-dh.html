Notes

This lesson has three parts: the actuators that move the joints, the sensors that tell the controller what is happening, and the Denavit–Hartenberg convention that describes where the joints put the tool.

Part A. Actuators and transmissions

Actuators move the joints, supply power and do work. In a serial robot the actuators near the base must carry the actuators of the outer links, so actuators must be light; parallel robots often keep all actuators at the base. An actuator drives its joint through a transmission. Three kinds are used: hydraulic, pneumatic and electric.

Stepper motors (permanent magnet, variable reluctance or hybrid) turn one step, typically 1.8° or 0.9°, for each pulse, as the rotor teeth line up with the energised stator poles. Speed is set by the pulse frequency and direction by the pulse sequence. They can run open loop because the error never exceeds one step, unless the load makes the motor slip. A two-phase stepper has four drive modes: wave (one phase on, less torque), full step (both phases on, rated torque), half step (alternating, half the step angle) and micro-stepping (currents varied smoothly, smaller steps, less torque). Choose a stepper from the load, friction and inertia, its torque–speed curve (holding torque, pull-in and pull-out curves) and its maximum slew rate. Steppers suit small robots with small loads and low speeds.

DC servo motors have a permanent-magnet rotor (rare-earth magnets such as samarium–cobalt or neodymium give high torque) and are commutated by brushes or, in brushless motors, by Hall sensors and electronics. An optical encoder or a tachogenerator on the shaft closes the loop. Brushless AC and DC servos have low friction, need little maintenance and are robust and cheap.

The torque is proportional to the current and the back emf to the speed:

$$T_m = K_t\, i_a, \qquad L_a\frac{di_a}{dt} + R_a i_a + K_g\dot\theta_m = V_a, \qquad J\ddot\theta_m + B\dot\theta_m = K_t i_a$$

Taking Laplace transforms gives the speed transfer function, a second-order system you met in the control courses:

$$\frac{\Omega_m(s)}{V_a(s)} = \frac{K_t}{(L_a s + R_a)(J s + B) + K_t K_g}$$

A PWM (pulse-width modulation) drive switches the supply on and off quickly; the duty ratio $D$ sets the average voltage $D\,V_s$. An H-bridge of four MOSFETs with diodes reverses the voltage: input A switches the diagonal pair TR1–TR4 (forward), input B the other diagonal TR2–TR3 (reverse). A and B together would short the supply through one side of the bridge, so it is not allowed. PWM plus an H-bridge controls both speed and direction.

The truth table on the slide gives "TR2 & TR4" for input B. Reverse needs the other diagonal, TR2 and TR3; TR2 with TR4 would short the supply.

Transmissions transfer power from the motor to the joint at a suitable speed, can turn rotation into translation, and carry motion to other joints and directions. A DC motor's rated speed is typically 1800 to 3600 rpm. At 3000 rpm the shaft turns at $3000/60 = 50$ rev/s $= 314$ rad/s; driving a 1 m link directly would move its tip at about 314 m/s, close to the speed of sound (343 m/s). A large speed reduction is needed.

The slide says "3000 rpm = 60 rps ≈ 360 rad/s" and gives a tip speed of 36 m/s "greater than the speed of sound". The correct figures are 50 rev/s, 314 rad/s and 314 m/s, which is just below the speed of sound; the point of the slide (a big reduction is needed) stands.

Robot transmissions must be stiff, light, free of backlash and efficient. Options: direct drive (motor on the joint: low friction and backlash, but expensive); gearboxes (spur, worm and wheel, planetary); belts and chains; harmonic drives for large reductions in one stage; ball screws and rack-and-pinion to turn rotation into translation; linkages such as the four-bar.

Part B. Sensors

A robot without sensors is like a person without eyes, ears or touch. It can only play back a taught program, needs costly and slow programming, and cannot cope with any change in its surroundings, tools or parts. Sensors let a robot infer the state of its environment, navigate and locate itself, respond flexibly and learn.

Why feedback needs sensors. In open loop, $Y = G R$, so a modelling error $\delta G/G$ of $x\,\%$ gives the same $x\,\%$ error in the output. With feedback and a controller $D(s)$,

$$Y = \frac{DG}{1 + DG}R, \qquad \frac{\delta Y}{Y} = \frac{1}{1 + DG}\,\frac{\delta G}{G}$$

so with a large loop gain the same model error has almost no effect: feedback lets cheaper models and controllers give robust performance. But this works only if the sensor is good.

With noise $N$ added by the sensor, $Y = \dfrac{DG}{1 + DG}(R - N)$: the output follows the corrupted signal, and no amount of loop gain removes the noise. The loop can be no better than its sensor.

Desirable sensor features: high accuracy and precision, linear response, wide range, fast response, easy calibration, reliable and rugged, cheap and easy to use. Sensors are internal (the robot's own joint variables, for control) or external (the environment, for guidance, identifying objects and handling material).

Internal sensors.

  • Joint position: incremental and absolute encoders (optical, magnetic, capacitive), potentiometers, linear resistive or digital scales.
  • Joint velocity: DC tachogenerators, resolvers, encoders (counting pulses per unit time).
  • Force and torque: at the joint actuator, at the wrist (the six components of force and moment on the environment) and at the end effector (grip force).

The optical encoder is the most widely used internal sensor. An etched disc between LEDs and photodiodes turns rotation into square waves. Glass discs give high resolution (11 to more than 16 bits); plastic (Mylar) or metal discs are more rugged (8 to 10 bits). A counter reads A, B and I and gives rotation and direction; the encoder can be absolute or relative and also gives speed.

Sizing an encoder: to place the tip of a link of length $L$ within $e$, the joint must resolve the angle $e/L$, so the encoder needs $2\pi/(e/L)$ counts per revolution, rounded up to a power of 2. For $L = 1$ m and $e = 0.5$ mm: $2\pi/(5\times10^{-4}) = 12\,566$ counts; $2^{13} = 8192$ is too few, $2^{14} = 16\,384$ is enough, so a 14-bit encoder. Shorter links need fewer bits; 12 bits are usually enough for small arms.

The slide rounds $2\pi/(Le)$ to about 10 000; the exact value is 12 566. The answer, 14 bits, is unchanged. (The expression should read $2\pi L/e$, i.e. $2\pi$ divided by the angle $e/L$.)

Potentiometers are cheap but not very accurate, add friction and suit slow joints. A resolver is a rotary transformer whose stator voltage is proportional to $\sin\theta$ of the rotor angle; its analog output needs an ADC. A tachometer gives a voltage proportional to speed.

Force/torque sensing. A joint sensor uses strain gauges on a compliant shaft, but the added compliance changes the robot's dynamics; for a DC motor the torque can also be estimated from the armature current, but not very accurately. A wrist force/torque sensor sits between the arm and the end effector and measures all six components $\mathbf F = (F_x, F_y, F_z, M_x, M_y, M_z)^T$ from (typically) eight strain-gauge readings $\mathbf w$: $\mathbf F = R_F\,\mathbf w$, where the $6\times 8$ matrix $R_F$ is found by calibration with least squares. It must be stiff, compact, close to the tool, linear, and machined from one block of metal to avoid hysteresis.

External sensors.

  • Touch: micro-switches, or an LED–photodiode pair that detects whether an object is between the fingers.
  • Slip: a free dimpled ball turned by a slipping object moves a rod and a conductive disc over a ring of contacts; the order of the contacts gives the direction of slip.
  • Tactile arrays ("artificial skin"): rows and columns of conductors; local pressure changes the resistance or capacitance at a crossing, giving the shape of the grasped object and the grip force. Other versions use fluid-filled membranes, Hall-sensor arrays or MEMS strain gauges.
  • Proximity (very short range, below about 15–20 mm): inductive (a coil and magnet; detects ferrous metal by the change in flux; used in metal detectors and traffic-light loops), Hall effect (the field through the sensor weakens near ferromagnetic material; ignition timing, ABS, brushless motors), capacitive (detects metals and non-metals; detects level through a container wall), ultrasonic (one transducer sends and receives; works in dirty environments, liquid level, parking sensors) and optical (LED and photodiode; level control, jam detection, counting boxes).
  • Range: triangulation, structured light, time of flight and vision (below).
  • Others: colour; speed and motion (Doppler, camera, accelerometer, gyroscope); identification (camera, RFID); localisation (compass, odometer, GPS).

Triangulation sweeps a narrow beam across the object; the detector output peaks when the lit spot is in front of it, and $D = B\tan\theta$. Little computation, but slow (one point at a time). Structured light projects a sheet of light; a camera slightly offset sees a stripe whose distortion gives the depth profile of a whole line at once (needs calibration and a scene where projected light is allowed).

Time of flight measures the round-trip time $t$ of a pulse: $D = ct/2$. With light, resolving 0.25 inch (6.35 mm) needs timing to about 40 ps, so pulsed light suits long distances; ultrasound ($c \approx 343$ m/s) is easy to time and is the usual choice for robot navigation and obstacle avoidance, but it spreads, reflects poorly from small objects, corners and some plastics. A continuous laser measures the phase shift $\theta$ instead: $D = \dfrac{\theta}{360°}\dfrac{\lambda}{2}$, unique only for $2D < \lambda$. Modulating the beam at 10 MHz gives $\lambda = c/f = 30$ m, so distances up to 15 m can be measured; the method also gives intensity and needs little computation.

Vision is the most powerful and most complex sensing: one or more cameras with processing that can find distance, shape, size, colour and brightness and support navigation, obstacle avoidance, position feedback and part location. It has six stages (sensing, pre-processing, segmentation, description, recognition, interpretation) at three levels: low (sensing and pre-processing, no intelligence), medium (segmentation, description and recognition of objects) and high (trying to emulate cognition).

New sensor technologies. MEMS sensors put tiny mechanical and electronic parts on one silicon chip; capacitive sensing is the most successful (no exotic materials, low power, stable over temperature). In a MEMS accelerometer a proof mass on polysilicon springs carries fingers between fixed plates; acceleration moves the mass and changes the differential capacitance. They are in cars, phones, hard disks and now mobile robots. MOEMS add optical detection and robust materials such as SiC for harsh conditions and are immune to electromagnetic and radio interference. A smart sensor is, by IEEE 1451.2, "a transducer that provides functions beyond those necessary for generating a correct representation of a sensed or controlled quantity": built-in processing (linearisation, engineering units, diagnostics), digital networking, and a TEDS (transducer electronic data sheet) for plug-and-play. Intelligent sensors add embedded algorithms that detect noise and sensor faults and take predefined actions, taking load off the robot controller.

A humanoid such as Honda's ASIMO shows what sensors make possible: vision, speed, balance, force, joint-angle and foot sensors on 34 servo-driven degrees of freedom let it walk, run, climb stairs, avoid obstacles and recognise faces and gestures.

Part C. The Denavit–Hartenberg convention

To compute where the tool is, attach a coordinate frame to every link and describe how each frame sits relative to the previous one. In general that needs six numbers per link. Denavit and Hartenberg (1955) showed that if the frames are placed by a few rules, four parameters are enough.

Rules for the frames (standard convention):

  1. Number the joints from the base. Put $z_{i-1}$ along the axis of joint $i$ (the rotation axis of a revolute joint, by the right-hand rule, or the direction of travel of a prismatic joint). The $y$ axis is never needed; it completes a right-handed frame.
  2. Find the common normal, the line perpendicular to both $z_{i-1}$ and $z_i$. The origin $O_i$ is where it meets $z_i$, and $x_i$ points along it, away from $z_{i-1}$.
  3. If $z_{i-1}$ and $z_i$ are parallel, there are infinitely many common normals: pick the one that makes the most parameters zero. If they intersect, the common normal has zero length ($a_i = 0$): put $x_i$ perpendicular to the plane of the two axes.
ParameterNameMeasured
$\theta_i$joint angleabout $z_{i-1}$, from $x_{i-1}$ to $x_i$ (the variable of a revolute joint)
$d_i$link offsetalong $z_{i-1}$, from $O_{i-1}$ to the common normal (the variable of a prismatic joint)
$a_i$link lengthalong $x_i$, the length of the common normal
$\alpha_i$link twistabout $x_i$, from $z_{i-1}$ to $z_i$

Only $\theta$ (revolute) or $d$ (prismatic) varies; the other three are fixed by the robot's geometry. Frame $i-1$ is carried to frame $i$ by four motions: rotate $\theta_i$ about $z$, translate $d_i$ along $z$, translate $a_i$ along $x$, rotate $\alpha_i$ about $x$:

$$A_i = \text{Rot}_z(\theta_i)\,\text{Trans}_z(d_i)\,\text{Trans}_x(a_i)\,\text{Rot}_x(\alpha_i) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta_i & -\sin\theta_i\cos\alpha_i & \sin\theta_i\sin\alpha_i & a_i\cos\theta_i \\ \sin\theta_i & \cos\theta_i\cos\alpha_i & -\cos\theta_i\sin\alpha_i & a_i\sin\theta_i \\ 0 & \sin\alpha_i & \cos\alpha_i & d_i \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$$

The forward kinematics is the product $T = A_1A_2\cdots A_n$: it maps tool coordinates into base (world) coordinates. Each row of the D-H table can describe the robot (with symbols) or one pose of it (with numbers).

The Chapter 4B slides mix two conventions: the parameter list and the robot examples use the standard (Spong) convention above, while the "Denavit–Hartenberg matrix" and the three-joint example use the modified (Craig) convention, where the parameters are $\alpha_{i-1}, a_{i-1}, d_i, \theta_i$ and the order of the motions is reversed. Both work, but never mix them in one table. Lesson 5 shows the modified form.

Inverse kinematics

Forward kinematics goes from joint values to the tool pose and always has one answer. Inverse kinematics goes the other way: find the joint values that put the tool at a desired position and orientation. It is harder: the equations are nonlinear, and there may be several solutions, one, or none (target outside the workspace).

For the two-link arm, the law of cosines in the triangle base–elbow–tip gives

$$\cos\theta_2 = \frac{x^2 + y^2 - a_1^2 - a_2^2}{2a_1a_2}, \qquad \theta_2 = \pm\arccos(\cdot), \qquad \theta_1 = \operatorname{atan2}(y, x) - \operatorname{atan2}(a_2\sin\theta_2,\; a_1 + a_2\cos\theta_2)$$

The two signs are the elbow-up and elbow-down solutions. Check with the example above: $x = 0.866$, $y = 1$ gives $\cos\theta_2 = (1.75 - 1.25)/1 = 0.5$, so $\theta_2 = \pm 60°$. With $+60°$: $\theta_1 = 49.1° - 19.1° = 30°$, the original pose. With $-60°$: $\theta_1 = 49.1° + 19.1° = 68.2°$, the mirror pose that reaches the same point. If $|\cos\theta_2| > 1$ the point is out of reach.

For a six-joint arm the same idea is applied systematically: write $T = A_1A_2\cdots A_6$ equal to the desired $\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf n & \mathbf o & \mathbf a & \mathbf p\end{bmatrix}$ matrix, then pre-multiply both sides by $A_1^{-1}$, $A_2^{-1}$, … and pick out elements that contain only one unknown. For the articulated arm of the slides this gives, for example, $\theta_1 = \operatorname{atan2}(p_y, p_x)$, then $\theta_3$, $\theta_2$, $\theta_4 = \theta_{234} - \theta_2 - \theta_3$, $\theta_5$ and $\theta_6$ in turn.

Straight-line motion. To move the tool along a straight line the controller breaks the line into many short segments and solves the inverse kinematics at each point, typically 50 to 200 times a second. The calculation must therefore be fast: remove every unnecessary computation.

Degeneracy and dexterity. A robot is degenerate (at a singularity) when it loses a degree of freedom and cannot move as desired: when joints reach their limits, or when the $z$ axes of two similar joints line up inside the workspace. Dexterity concerns the points where the robot can be placed but not oriented as desired.

A known weakness of D-H: every motion is about or along $x$ and $z$, never $y$. When two consecutive joint axes are nominally parallel but slightly misaligned, the common normal jumps, so tiny manufacturing errors give huge parameter changes. Calibration methods and the product-of-exponentials form in Lesson 5 avoid this.

Exercises

  1. A two-phase stepper motor has a step angle of 1.8° and is driven at 400 pulses per second. Find its speed in full-step and in half-step mode.
    Show solution
    Full step: $400 \times 1.8° = 720°$/s $= 2$ rev/s $= 120$ rpm. Half step (0.9° per pulse): 60 rpm, with twice the positioning resolution.
  2. A DC servo has $K_t = 0.05$ N·m/A and $K_g = 0.05$ V·s/rad, $R_a = 1\ \Omega$, negligible $L_a$. At 2 A, what torque does it give? At 300 rad/s, what back emf? What supply voltage keeps it at 300 rad/s while drawing 2 A?
    Show solution
    Torque $= 0.05 \times 2 = 0.1$ N·m. Back emf $= 0.05 \times 300 = 15$ V. Supply $V_a = R_a i_a + K_g\dot\theta = 2 + 15 = 17$ V.
  3. A link is 0.5 m long and the tip must be positioned to 0.1 mm. How many bits does the joint encoder need?
    Show solution
    Angle $= e/L = 10^{-4}/0.5 = 2\times10^{-4}$ rad. Counts $= 2\pi/(2\times10^{-4}) = 31\,416$. $2^{15} = 32\,768$ is enough, so a 15-bit encoder.
  4. An ultrasonic range sensor hears the echo 5.8 ms after the pulse. How far is the object? A triangulation sensor has a 0.2 m baseline and sees the spot at 60°; how far is that object?
    Show solution
    Time of flight: $D = ct/2 = 343 \times 0.0058/2 = 0.99$ m. Triangulation: $D = 0.2\tan 60° = 0.346$ m.
  5. For the two-link arm with $a_1 = a_2 = 1$ m, find the tip position for $\theta_1 = 45°$, $\theta_2 = -90°$.
    Show solution
    $x = \cos 45° + \cos(-45°) = 1.414$ m, $y = \sin 45° + \sin(-45°) = 0$. The tip is on the $x$ axis.
  6. For $a_1 = 1$ m, $a_2 = 0.5$ m, find both inverse kinematics solutions that put the tip at $(1.2, 0.6)$ m. Check one of them with the simulation.
    Show solution
    $\cos\theta_2 = (1.44 + 0.36 - 1 - 0.25)/1 = 0.55$, so $\theta_2 = \pm 56.6°$. $\operatorname{atan2}(0.6, 1.2) = 26.6°$; $\operatorname{atan2}(0.5\sin 56.6°, 1 + 0.5\cos 56.6°) = \operatorname{atan2}(0.418, 1.275) = 18.1°$. Elbow up: $\theta_1 = 8.4°$, $\theta_2 = 56.6°$. Elbow down: $\theta_1 = 44.7°$, $\theta_2 = -56.6°$.
  7. Fill in the D-H table for a planar RP arm: a revolute joint at the base, then a prismatic joint that slides along the arm. Write $T$.
    Show solution
    Link 1: $a_1 = 0$, $\alpha_1 = 90°$, $d_1 = 0$, $\theta_1^*$ (the twist turns $z_1$ to lie along the arm). Link 2: $a_2 = 0$, $\alpha_2 = 0$, $d_2^*$, $\theta_2 = 0$. Then the tip is at $x = d_2\cos\theta_1$, $y = d_2\sin\theta_1$: the last column of $T = A_1A_2$ is $(d_2c_1,\ d_2s_1,\ 0,\ 1)^T$ (with $z_0$ out of the page and the arm in the $x_0y_0$ plane).
