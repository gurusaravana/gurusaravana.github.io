Notes

Feedforward control

Feedback waits for an error. If a disturbance $d$ can be measured, a feedforward controller can act before the CV moves. For process $G_p$ and disturbance path $G_d$, perfect cancellation needs

$$G_{ff}(s) = -\frac{G_d(s)}{G_p(s)}$$

For FOPDT models this becomes a gain, a lead–lag and a delay. A static feedforward (gain only, $-K_d/K_p$) is often enough. Feedforward is always combined with feedback, which cleans up model errors.

Cascade control

A cascade uses two controllers: the outer (primary) controller sets the set-point of an inner (secondary) controller. Example: a reactor temperature controller (outer) sets the set-point of a jacket temperature or coolant flow controller (inner).

  • The inner loop fixes disturbances in the inner part (for example coolant supply pressure) before they reach the outer variable.
  • It works best when the inner loop is at least 3–5 times faster than the outer loop.
  • Tune the inner loop first with the outer loop in manual, then put the inner loop in cascade and tune the outer loop.

Exercises

  1. $G_p = 2/(10s + 1)$ and $G_d = 0.5/(10s + 1)$. Design the feedforward controller.
    Show solution
    $G_{ff} = -G_d/G_p = -0.25$: a pure static gain because the dynamics are the same.
  2. Give one industrial example of cascade control and name the primary and secondary variables.
    Show solution
    Steam-heated exchanger: primary is outlet process temperature, secondary is steam flow (or steam pressure).
  3. Why must the inner loop of a cascade be faster than the outer loop?
    Show solution
    So that, from the outer controller's view, the inner loop follows its set-point almost instantly. If the speeds are similar the two loops interact and may oscillate.
