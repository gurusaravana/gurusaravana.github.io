  • Choose between tight and averaging level control

Notes

This lesson ties the course together on the tank from Lesson 2, which you can operate in the simulation below.

Step 1: open-loop behaviour

Put the simulation in Manual. Step the inlet valve from 50 % to 60 % and estimate $K$ and $\tau$ from the level trend (Lesson 5). Repeat at a different operating level: the parameters change because the tank is nonlinear (Lesson 3).

Step 2: tuning

Use IMC rules (Lesson 10) with your model to choose $K_c$ and $\tau_I$, then switch to Auto.

Step 3: disturbance test

Open the outlet valve from 50 % to 80 %. In Manual the level falls to a new steady state. In Auto the controller opens the inlet valve and brings the level back.

Tight versus averaging level control

  • Tight control keeps the level close to the set-point, for example for a reactor where volume sets the residence time. The outflow changes quickly.
  • Averaging control lets the level swing inside limits so that the outflow to the next unit changes smoothly. Use a low gain and long integral time. A surge tank exists for this purpose.

Exercises

  1. Run the step test in Manual. Report your $K$ (m per % valve) and $\tau$.
    Show solution
    Answers depend on the operating point. stepping the inlet from 50 % to 60 % with the outlet at 50 % moves the level from about 2.8 m to 4.0 m, so $K \approx 0.12$ m/%, and $\tau$ is roughly 30 s.
  2. With $K_c = 5$ and $\tau_I = 60$ s, then $K_c = 50$ and $\tau_I = 5$ s, describe the responses to the outlet disturbance.
    Show solution
    The first is sluggish: the level drifts a long way before recovering (averaging). The second is fast but oscillatory and moves the inlet valve aggressively (tight).
  3. Which is more appropriate for a surge tank feeding a distillation column, and why?
    Show solution
    Averaging control, to pass smooth flow changes to the column, which is sensitive to feed rate swings.
