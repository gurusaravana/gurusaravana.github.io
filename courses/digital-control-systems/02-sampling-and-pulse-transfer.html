Notes

Lesson 1 worked entirely with sequences. A real digital loop also contains a continuous plant, so we need two bridges: a rule for how fast to sample, and models of the ADC and DAC that let us describe "DAC, plant, ADC" as one z-domain transfer function.

The sampling theorem

Sampling is needed to process analog data with digital elements. For it to work, the samples must capture the nature of the analog signal, and the analog signal must be recoverable from its samples.

A signal is band limited when its spectrum is zero above some frequency $\omega_m$:

$$f(t) \;\xrightarrow{\ \mathcal{F}\ }\; F(j\omega), \qquad F(j\omega) \ne 0 \text{ for } -\omega_m \le \omega \le \omega_m, \qquad F(j\omega) = 0 \text{ elsewhere}$$

Sampling theorem. Such a signal can be reconstructed from its impulse-sampled version $f^*(t) = \sum_k f(t)\,\delta(t - kT)$ if and only if the sampling frequency $\omega_s = 2\pi/T$ satisfies

$$\omega_s > 2\omega_m$$

This is the same Nyquist condition we met in the s-to-z mapping: every frequency in the signal must lie inside the primary strip $|\omega| < \pi/T = \omega_s/2$.

Choosing the sampling frequency

Physical signals are not strictly band limited, but most have an effective bandwidth beyond which the spectrum is negligible. In practice we sample much faster than the theorem's lower bound. A rule of thumb is

$$\omega_s = k\,\omega_m, \qquad 5 \le k \le 10$$

The choice of $k$ depends on the application. Two things limit how fast we can go:

  • Sensor delay. The loop cannot sample faster than the output can be measured. Oxygen sensors in automotive air/fuel ratio control take about 20 ms, which caps the sampling frequency at 50 Hz.
  • Computation time. The control law must be computed between samples. Faster processors make this less restrictive, but it must still be checked.

For a linear system the output spectrum is the input spectrum times the frequency response. The input is not known in advance, so we base the choice on the system's frequency response.

First-order systems

A first-order system $H(j\omega) = \dfrac{K}{j\omega/\omega_b + 1}$ has DC gain $K$ and bandwidth $\omega_b = 1/\tau$, where $\tau$ is the time constant ($f_{3\text{dB}} = 1/(2\pi\tau)$).

At $7\omega_b$ the gain has fallen to $1/\sqrt{50} \approx 0.14$ of its DC value (−17 dB), which is small enough to treat as the edge of the significant spectrum. Taking $\omega_m = 7\omega_b$ and $k = 5$ to 10 gives

$$\omega_s = k\,\omega_b, \qquad 35 \le k \le 70$$

The slides say the gain drops "by a factor of about 10" at $7\omega_b$. The exact figure is $1/\sqrt{1 + 49} \approx 1/7$, as the slide's own Bode plot shows (−16.9 dB). The rule $35 \le k \le 70$ is unaffected. The slides also write $\omega_b = 1/T$; here $T$ means the time constant, written $\tau$ on this page so it is not confused with the sampling period.

Second-order systems

For $H(j\omega) = \dfrac{K}{1 - (\omega/\omega_n)^2 + j2\zeta\omega/\omega_n}$ the bandwidth is approximately the damped natural frequency $\omega_d = \omega_n\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}$. Taking $7\omega_d$ as the highest significant frequency,

$$\omega_s = k\,\omega_d, \qquad 35 \le k \le 70$$

Example 1. A first-order system has bandwidth 10 rad/s. Choose $k = 60$: $\omega_s = 600$ rad/s and $T = 2\pi/600 = 0.0105$ s, so $T \approx 0.01$ s.

Example 2. A closed loop must have steady-state error at most 5%, $\zeta \approx 0.7$ and $\omega_n = 10$ rad/s. The sensor delay is 0.02 s. Choose a sampling period.

$$\omega_s \ge 35\omega_d = 35 \times 10\sqrt{1 - 0.7^2} = 249.95 \text{ rad/s} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T \le \frac{2\pi}{249.95} = 0.025 \text{ s}$$

$T = 20$ ms is a good choice: it meets the bound and equals the sensor delay, so every sample has a fresh measurement.

The slides write $2\pi$ as "2 × 3141". The value used is $2\pi \approx 6.283$.

The ADC model

Assume that the ADC output equals its input in magnitude (quantization error is negligible), that conversion is instantaneous, and that sampling is perfectly uniform. Then the ADC is an ideal sampler with period $T$.

$$u^*(t) = u(t)\,\delta_T(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} u(kT)\,\delta(t - kT)$$

In the language of modulation, $u(t)$ is the modulating signal, the impulse train $\delta_T(t)$ is the carrier, and $u^*(t)$ is the modulated wave.

The DAC model: the zero-order hold

Assume that the DAC output equals its input in magnitude, that conversion is instantaneous, and that the output is held constant over each sampling period. The DAC is then a zero-order hold (ZOH):

$$u_h(t) = u(k), \qquad kT \le t < (k+1)T$$

To get the transfer function, take the Laplace transform of the impulse response. The pulse is a unit step minus a unit step delayed by $T$, and $\mathcal{L}\{1(t)\} = 1/s$, $\mathcal{L}\{-1(t - T)\} = -e^{-sT}/s$, so

$$G_{ZOH}(s) = \frac{1 - e^{-sT}}{s}$$

Transfer function of the DAC, analog subsystem and ADC

Both the input and the output of the cascade in the dashed box are sampled, so it has a z-domain transfer function. With analog subsystem $G(s)$, the DAC and plant together are

$$G_{ZA}(s) = G_{ZOH}(s)\,G(s) = \frac{G(s)}{s} - \frac{G(s)}{s}e^{-sT}$$

Taking the inverse Laplace transform, with $g_s(t) = \mathcal{L}^{-1}\{G(s)/s\}$ the plant's step response,

$$g_{ZA}(t) = g_s(t) - g_s(t - T) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad g_{ZA}(kT) = g_s(kT) - g_s(kT - T)$$

The ADC samples this. Z-transforming, and using the delay property on the second term:

$$G_{ZAS}(z) = (1 - z^{-1})\,\mathcal{Z}\left\{\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left[\frac{G(s)}{s}\right]^*\right\} \quad\text{or, more briefly,}\quad G_{ZAS}(z) = (1 - z^{-1})\,\mathcal{Z}\left\{\frac{G(s)}{s}\right\}$$

The star is a reminder that the time function must be sampled before it is z-transformed. This $G_{ZAS}(z)$ is often called the pulse transfer function of the plant with a zero-order hold.

Recipe: divide $G(s)$ by $s$, expand in partial fractions, replace each term by its z-transform from the table, then multiply by $(z - 1)/z$.

Example 3: vehicle cruise control

Newton's law $M\dot{v} = u - bv$ gives $G(s) = \dfrac{V(s)}{U(s)} = \dfrac{1}{Ms + b} = \dfrac{K}{\tau s + 1} = \dfrac{K/\tau}{s + 1/\tau}$ with $K = 1/b$ and $\tau = M/b$. Then

$$\frac{G(s)}{s} = \frac{K/\tau}{s(s + 1/\tau)} = K\left[\frac{1}{s} - \frac{1}{s + 1/\tau}\right]$$

$$G_{ZAS}(z) = \frac{z - 1}{z}\,K\left[\frac{z}{z - 1} - \frac{z}{z - e^{-T/\tau}}\right] = K\left[1 - \frac{z - 1}{z - e^{-T/\tau}}\right] = K\,\frac{1 - e^{-T/\tau}}{z - e^{-T/\tau}}$$

A first-order plant stays first order, with its pole at $z = e^{-T/\tau}$, exactly the $z = e^{sT}$ image of $s = -1/\tau$.

Example 4: vehicle position control

With position $y$ as the output, $G(s) = \dfrac{Y(s)}{U(s)} = \dfrac{1}{s(Ms + b)} = \dfrac{K/\tau}{s(s + 1/\tau)}$. Now

$$\frac{G(s)}{s} = \frac{K/\tau}{s^2(s + 1/\tau)} = K\left[\frac{1}{s^2} - \frac{\tau}{s} + \frac{\tau}{s + 1/\tau}\right]$$

Using $\mathcal{Z}\{1/s^2\} = Tz/(z - 1)^2$ (the sampled ramp $kT$):

$$G_{ZAS}(z) = \frac{z - 1}{z}K\left[\frac{Tz}{(z - 1)^2} - \frac{\tau z}{z - 1} + \frac{\tau z}{z - e^{-T/\tau}}\right] = K\left[\frac{T}{z - 1} - \tau + \frac{\tau(z - 1)}{z - e^{-T/\tau}}\right]$$

$$G_{ZAS}(z) = K\,\frac{\left(T - \tau + \tau e^{-T/\tau}\right)z + \left(\tau - (\tau + T)e^{-T/\tau}\right)}{(z - 1)(z - e^{-T/\tau})}$$

The slides write $1$ where $T$ appears in the ramp term and in the final numerator, which is the result for $T = 1$ s only. The general form above keeps $T$.

Example 5: series R-L circuit

The voltage divider rule gives $\dfrac{V_o}{V_{in}} = \dfrac{Ls}{R + Ls} = \dfrac{\tau s}{1 + \tau s}$ with $\tau = L/R$. So $\dfrac{G(s)}{s} = \dfrac{1}{s + 1/\tau}$ and

$$G_{ZAS}(z) = (1 - z^{-1})\,\mathcal{Z}\left\{\frac{1}{s + 1/\tau}\right\} = \frac{z - 1}{z}\cdot\frac{z}{z - e^{-T/\tau}} = \frac{z - 1}{z - e^{-T/\tau}}$$

The zero at $z = 1$ reflects the fact that the inductor voltage is zero in steady state for a constant input.

Closed-loop systems

With a digital controller $C(z)$ and the plant written as $G_{ZAS}(z)$, the loop has exactly the same algebra as a continuous loop, with $s$ replaced by $z$:

$$G_{cl}(z) = \frac{Y(z)}{R(z)} = \frac{C(z)G_{ZAS}(z)}{1 + C(z)G_{ZAS}(z)}, \qquad \text{characteristic equation: } 1 + C(z)G_{ZAS}(z) = 0$$

The closed loop is stable when every root of the characteristic equation lies inside the unit circle. Stability tests for this condition are the subject of a later chapter.

Exercises

  1. Repeat Example 2 (steady-state error at most 5%, $\zeta \approx 0.7$, $\omega_n = 10$ rad/s) when the sensor delay is 0.03 s. Select a suitable sampling period.
    Show solution
    The rule of thumb still asks for $\omega_s \ge 35\omega_d = 249.95$ rad/s, that is $T \le 25$ ms. The sensor needs 30 ms, so $T$ cannot be shorter than 30 ms. With $T = 30$ ms, $\omega_s = 2\pi/0.03 = 209$ rad/s $\approx 29\,\omega_d$: a little below the 35 to 70 range but still far above the Nyquist limit ($2 \times 7\omega_d \approx 100$ rad/s). Choose $T = 30$ ms and check the closed-loop response, or use a faster sensor if the margin is not enough.
  2. A first-order process has time constant $\tau = 0.2$ s. Give the range of suitable sampling periods.
    Show solution
    $\omega_b = 1/\tau = 5$ rad/s, so $\omega_s = 35$ to $70 \times 5 = 175$ to $350$ rad/s. Then $T = 2\pi/\omega_s$ lies between $2\pi/350 = 18$ ms and $2\pi/175 = 36$ ms.
  3. Find $G_{ZAS}(z)$ for $G(s) = \dfrac{2}{s + 2}$ with $T = 0.1$ s.
    Show solution
    This is the cruise-control form with $K = 1$ and $\tau = 0.5$ s, so $G_{ZAS}(z) = \dfrac{1 - e^{-0.2}}{z - e^{-0.2}} = \dfrac{0.1813}{z - 0.8187}$. Its DC gain $G_{ZAS}(1) = 1$ matches $G(0) = 1$, as it must.
  4. Find $G_{ZAS}(z)$ for a pure integrator $G(s) = 1/s$.
    Show solution
    $G(s)/s = 1/s^2$, whose z-transform is $Tz/(z - 1)^2$. Then $G_{ZAS}(z) = \dfrac{z - 1}{z}\cdot\dfrac{Tz}{(z - 1)^2} = \dfrac{T}{z - 1}$: the familiar "add $T$ times the input every step" integrator, $y(k+1) = y(k) + Tu(k)$.
  5. A proportional controller $C(z) = K_p$ controls the plant of exercise 3. Find the characteristic equation and the range of $K_p > 0$ for which the closed loop is stable.
    Show solution
    $1 + K_p\dfrac{0.1813}{z - 0.8187} = 0$ gives $z = 0.8187 - 0.1813K_p$. Stability needs $|z| < 1$: $-1 < 0.8187 - 0.1813K_p < 1$, so $0 < K_p < 10.03$. The continuous loop would be stable for every $K_p > 0$; sampling and the hold add phase lag that limits the gain.
  6. Why does the zero-order hold make the staircase lag the smooth signal, and roughly by how much?
    Show solution
    Each value is held for a whole period after it is sampled, so on average the output is half a period old. The ZOH acts approximately like a time delay of $T/2$, which adds phase lag $\omega T/2$ at frequency $\omega$. This is one more reason to sample fast compared with the loop bandwidth.
