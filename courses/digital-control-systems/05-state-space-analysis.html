Notes

So far every model has been a transfer function. State-space models describe the same systems with first-order difference equations in vector form. They handle several inputs and outputs, expose the internal behaviour (controllability, observability) and lead to the design methods of the next lessons.

Discrete-time systems

A discrete-time system transforms an input sequence $u(k)$ into an output sequence $y(k)$. There are two kinds:

  • Inherently discrete systems, such as digital processors, where only the sampling instants matter and what happens in between is irrelevant.
  • Sampled continuous systems: a continuous plant viewed only at the sampling instants, usually together with its DAC and ADC.

A digital controller is a program that turns one sequence into another; it is a signal processor that happens to control a plant. Its state variable model has first-order difference equations for the state variables and an algebraic equation for the output. The input sequence $u(k)$ and the initial state $x(0)$ together fix all future behaviour.

The state variable model

$$x(k+1) = Fx(k) + gu(k), \qquad y(k) = cx(k) + du(k)$$

with $x(k)$ the $n \times 1$ state vector, $F$ the $n \times n$ state matrix, $g$ the $n \times 1$ input vector, $c$ the $1 \times n$ output vector and $d$ a scalar (direct transmission).

From state space to transfer function

Z-transform the state equation, keeping the initial state: $zX(z) - zx(0) = FX(z) + gU(z)$, so

$$X(z) = (zI - F)^{-1}z\,x(0) + (zI - F)^{-1}g\,U(z)$$

$$Y(z) = c(zI - F)^{-1}z\,x(0) + \left[c(zI - F)^{-1}g + d\right]U(z)$$

With $x(0) = 0$ the transfer function is

$$G(z) = \frac{Y(z)}{U(z)} = c(zI - F)^{-1}g + d = c\,\frac{\operatorname{adj}(zI - F)}{|zI - F|}\,g + d$$

The characteristic polynomial $|zI - F|$ has the eigenvalues of $F$ as roots. These are the poles of $G(z)$; for a system that is both controllable and observable (no cancellation), the eigenvalues of $F$ and the poles of $G(z)$ are exactly the same set.

Example 1. $x(k+1) = Ax(k) + Bu(k)$, $y(k) = Cx(k)$ with $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$, $C = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $D = 0$, $x(0) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. (a) Find the transfer function. (b) Find the step response.

$$(zI - A)^{-1} = \frac{1}{(z - 1)(z - 3)}\begin{bmatrix} z - 3 & 1 \\ 0 & z - 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad G(z) = \frac{\begin{bmatrix} 0 & z - 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}}{(z - 1)(z - 3)} = \frac{2(z - 1)}{(z - 1)(z - 3)} = \frac{2}{z - 3}$$

The eigenvalue $z = 1$ cancels: it cannot be seen from the output. For the step response with zero initial state,

$$Y(z) = \frac{2}{z - 3}\cdot\frac{z}{z - 1}, \qquad \frac{Y(z)}{z} = \frac{1}{z - 3} - \frac{1}{z - 1} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y(k) = 3^k - 1, \quad k \ge 0$$

The response has a transient term $3^k$ and a steady-state term from the step. Here the "transient" grows, because the pole at 3 is outside the unit circle.

The slides compute only this zero-state response, although they also give $x(0) = [1\ \ 1]^T$. The initial state adds $c(zI - A)^{-1}z\,x(0) = z/(z - 3)$, that is $3^k$, so the total step response is $y(k) = 2\cdot3^k - 1$. Check: $y(0) = x_2(0) = 1$ and $y(1) = 5$.

From transfer function to state space: canonical forms

Going from a transfer function to a state model is called realization. There are infinitely many realizations; three standard ("canonical") ones are especially useful. They make controllability, observability and stability easy to read, they map directly onto delay-adder-multiplier hardware or code, and they are the starting point for pole placement and controller design. All three start from

$$G(z) = \frac{\beta_0z^n + \beta_1z^{n-1} + \dots + \beta_{n-1}z + \beta_n}{z^n + \alpha_1z^{n-1} + \dots + \alpha_{n-1}z + \alpha_n}$$

First companion form (controllable canonical form)

$$x_1(k+1) = x_2(k), \quad \dots, \quad x_{n-1}(k+1) = x_n(k), \quad x_n(k+1) = -\alpha_nx_1(k) - \alpha_{n-1}x_2(k) - \dots - \alpha_1x_n(k) + u(k)$$

$$F = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & & & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 \\ -\alpha_n & -\alpha_{n-1} & -\alpha_{n-2} & \cdots & -\alpha_1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad g = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$c = \begin{bmatrix} \beta_n - \alpha_n\beta_0 & \beta_{n-1} - \alpha_{n-1}\beta_0 & \cdots & \beta_1 - \alpha_1\beta_0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad d = \beta_0$$

The last row of $F$ holds the denominator coefficients in reverse order; the output row holds the numerator coefficients. When $G(z)$ is strictly proper ($\beta_0 = 0$), $c = [\beta_n\ \ \beta_{n-1}\ \cdots\ \beta_1]$ and $d = 0$.

The slides give the output as $y(k) = \beta_0u(k) + \beta_1x_1(k) + \dots + \beta_nx_n(k)$, so $c = [\beta_1\ \cdots\ \beta_n]$. With $x_1$ the deepest delay, the coefficients run the other way ($\beta_n$ multiplies $x_1$), and when $\beta_0 \ne 0$ each one is reduced by $\alpha_i\beta_0$, as above.

Second companion form (observable canonical form)

$$\begin{aligned} x_1(k+1) &= -\alpha_n\left(x_n(k) + \beta_0u(k)\right) + \beta_nu(k) \\ x_2(k+1) &= x_1(k) - \alpha_{n-1}\left(x_n(k) + \beta_0u(k)\right) + \beta_{n-1}u(k) \\ &\ \ \vdots \\ x_n(k+1) &= x_{n-1}(k) - \alpha_1\left(x_n(k) + \beta_0u(k)\right) + \beta_1u(k) \\ y(k) &= x_n(k) + \beta_0u(k) \end{aligned}$$

$$F = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & -\alpha_n \\ 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & -\alpha_{n-1} \\ 0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 & -\alpha_{n-2} \\ \vdots & & \ddots & & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 & -\alpha_1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad g = \begin{bmatrix} \beta_n - \alpha_n\beta_0 \\ \beta_{n-1} - \alpha_{n-1}\beta_0 \\ \vdots \\ \beta_1 - \alpha_1\beta_0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad c = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \cdots & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad d = \beta_0$$

This $F$ is the transpose of the first companion matrix, and $g$, $c$ swap roles: the two forms are duals.

The slides print the same $F$ (last row $-\alpha_i$) for both companion forms. The equations of the second form above give ones on the sub-diagonal and the $-\alpha_i$ in the last column, the transpose of the first form's matrix.

Jordan canonical form

Split off the direct term, $G(z) = \beta_0 + G'(z)$, and expand $G'(z)$ in partial fractions. With distinct poles $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$:

$$G(z) = \beta_0 + \frac{r_1}{z - \lambda_1} + \frac{r_2}{z - \lambda_2} + \dots + \frac{r_n}{z - \lambda_n}$$

$$\Lambda = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & \lambda_n \end{bmatrix}, \quad g = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ \vdots \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad c = \begin{bmatrix} r_1 & r_2 & \cdots & r_n \end{bmatrix}, \quad d = \beta_0$$

Each state is one mode of the system, which makes stability obvious: the system is stable when every $|\lambda_i| < 1$.

Repeated poles. If $\lambda_1$ has multiplicity $m$, its part of the expansion is $H_1(z) = \dfrac{r_{11}}{(z - \lambda_1)^m} + \dfrac{r_{12}}{(z - \lambda_1)^{m-1}} + \dots + \dfrac{r_{1m}}{z - \lambda_1}$. Its states form a chain (the output of one delay loop feeds the next), and $\Lambda$ becomes block diagonal, $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\Lambda_1, \lambda_{m+1}, \dots, \lambda_n)$, with the $m \times m$ Jordan block

$$\Lambda_1 = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1 & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_1 & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & & \ddots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & \lambda_1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & \lambda_1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad g = [0\ \cdots\ 0\ 1 \mid 1\ \cdots\ 1]^T, \quad c = [r_{11}\ \cdots\ r_{1m} \mid r_{m+1}\ \cdots\ r_n]$$

Example 2. Realize $G(z) = \dfrac{2z + 1}{z^2 + 0.3z + 0.02}$ in all three forms. Here $\beta_0 = 0$, $\beta_1 = 2$, $\beta_2 = 1$, $\alpha_1 = 0.3$, $\alpha_2 = 0.02$, and the poles are $-0.1$ and $-0.2$.

For the Jordan form, $r_1 = \left.\dfrac{2z + 1}{z + 0.2}\right|_{z=-0.1} = 8$ and $r_2 = \left.\dfrac{2z + 1}{z + 0.1}\right|_{z=-0.2} = -6$. All three give the same $G(z)$, with $d = 0$.

Solving the state difference equation

Given $x(k+1) = Fx(k) + gu(k)$ and $x(0)$, simply iterate:

$$x(1) = Fx(0) + gu(0), \quad x(2) = F^2x(0) + Fgu(0) + gu(1), \quad x(3) = F^3x(0) + F^2gu(0) + Fgu(1) + gu(2)$$

The pattern gives the closed form

$$x(k) = F^kx(0) + \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} F^{k-1-i}g\,u(i)$$

The first term is the contribution of the initial state (the free response); the second is the accumulated contribution of the inputs, each $u(i)$ propagated forward $k - 1 - i$ steps.

The state transition matrix

$\Phi(k) = F^k$, with $\Phi(0) = I$, is the state transition matrix. It carries the state from $x(0)$ to $x(k)$ when there is no input, $x(k) = \Phi(k)x(0)$, and it captures the dynamics of the system independent of the input. In its terms,

$$x(k) = \Phi(k)x(0) + \sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\Phi(k - 1 - i)\,g\,u(i)$$

Computing $F^k$ with the z-transform. With $u = 0$, $zX(z) - zx(0) = FX(z)$, so $X(z) = (zI - F)^{-1}z\,x(0)$. Comparing with $x(k) = F^kx(0)$:

$$\Phi(k) = F^k = \mathcal{Z}^{-1}\left[(zI - F)^{-1}z\right]$$

$(zI - F)^{-1}$ is called the resolvent matrix. Expand each entry of $(zI - F)^{-1}z$ in partial fractions and invert term by term.

Example 3. $F = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -0.16 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$. Then $|zI - F| = z^2 + z + 0.16 = (z + 0.2)(z + 0.8)$ and

$$(zI - F)^{-1} = \frac{1}{(z + 0.2)(z + 0.8)}\begin{bmatrix} z + 1 & 1 \\ -0.16 & z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{4/3}{z + 0.2} - \dfrac{1/3}{z + 0.8} & \dfrac{5/3}{z + 0.2} - \dfrac{5/3}{z + 0.8} \\[2mm] \dfrac{-0.8/3}{z + 0.2} + \dfrac{0.8/3}{z + 0.8} & \dfrac{-1/3}{z + 0.2} + \dfrac{4/3}{z + 0.8} \end{bmatrix}$$

Multiplying by $z$ and inverting each $z/(z + a)$ to $(-a)^k$:

$$\Phi(k) = F^k = \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac43(-0.2)^k - \tfrac13(-0.8)^k & \tfrac53(-0.2)^k - \tfrac53(-0.8)^k \\[1mm] -\tfrac{0.8}{3}(-0.2)^k + \tfrac{0.8}{3}(-0.8)^k & -\tfrac13(-0.2)^k + \tfrac43(-0.8)^k \end{bmatrix}$$

Check: at $k = 0$ this is the identity, and at $k = 1$ it is $F$.

Example 4. $x(k+1) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -2 & -3 \end{bmatrix}x(k) + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}(-1)^k$, $x_1(0) = x_2(0) = 1$, $y(k) = x_1(k)$. Find $y(k)$.

The eigenvalues are $-1$ and $-2$, and the same method gives

$$\Phi(k) = \begin{bmatrix} 2(-1)^k - (-2)^k & (-1)^k - (-2)^k \\ -2(-1)^k + 2(-2)^k & -(-1)^k + 2(-2)^k \end{bmatrix}$$

The free part of $x_1$ is $\Phi_{11} + \Phi_{12} = 3(-1)^k - 2(-2)^k$. The forced part is $\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\Phi_{12}(k-1-i)(-1)^i = \sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\left[(-1)^{k-1-i} - (-2)^{k-1-i}\right](-1)^i$. The first sum is $k(-1)^{k-1}$; the second is $(-2)^{k-1}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(1/2)^i = -(-2)^k\left[1 - (1/2)^k\right]$ with the sign pulled out. Collecting terms:

$$y(k) = 3(-1)^k - 2(-2)^k - k(-1)^k + (-2)^k - (-1)^k = (2 - k)(-1)^k - (-2)^k$$

Check by iteration: $y = 1, 1, -4, 9, -18, 35, \dots$ for $k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$, which matches the formula.

Exercises

  1. Find the transfer function of $x(k+1) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -0.5 & 1.5 \end{bmatrix}x(k) + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}u(k)$, $y(k) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}x(k)$. Is the system asymptotically stable?
    Show solution
    This is the first companion form with $\alpha_1 = -1.5$, $\alpha_2 = 0.5$ and $c = [\beta_2\ \ \beta_1] = [1\ \ 0]$, so $G(z) = \dfrac{1}{z^2 - 1.5z + 0.5} = \dfrac{1}{(z - 1)(z - 0.5)}$. The eigenvalues of $F$ are $1$ and $0.5$. The pole on the unit circle makes it marginally stable, not asymptotically stable.
  2. Realize $G(z) = \dfrac{z + 0.5}{z^2 - 0.5z + 0.06}$ in the first companion, second companion and Jordan forms.
    Show solution
    $\alpha_1 = -0.5$, $\alpha_2 = 0.06$, $\beta_0 = 0$, $\beta_1 = 1$, $\beta_2 = 0.5$; the poles are $0.2$ and $0.3$.
    First companion: $F = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -0.06 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix}$, $g = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $c = [0.5\ \ 1]$.
    Second companion: $F = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -0.06 \\ 1 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix}$, $g = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $c = [0\ \ 1]$.
    Jordan: $G(z) = \dfrac{-7}{z - 0.2} + \dfrac{8}{z - 0.3}$, so $\Lambda = \mathrm{diag}(0.2,\ 0.3)$, $g = [1\ \ 1]^T$, $c = [-7\ \ 8]$.
  3. Realize $G(z) = \dfrac{z^2 + 1}{z^2 - 0.5z + 0.06}$ in the first companion form.
    Show solution
    Now $\beta_0 = 1$, $\beta_1 = 0$, $\beta_2 = 1$. $F$ and $g$ are as in the previous problem; $c = [\beta_2 - \alpha_2\beta_0\ \ \ \beta_1 - \alpha_1\beta_0] = [0.94\ \ 0.5]$ and $d = 1$. Check: $G(z) = 1 + \dfrac{0.5z + 0.94}{z^2 - 0.5z + 0.06}$, which recombines to the given fraction.
  4. Find the state transition matrix $\Phi(k)$ for $F = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -0.06 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix}$ and check it at $k = 0$ and $k = 1$.
    Show solution
    $(zI - F)^{-1} = \dfrac{1}{(z - 0.2)(z - 0.3)}\begin{bmatrix} z - 0.5 & 1 \\ -0.06 & z \end{bmatrix}$. Expand $z(zI - F)^{-1}$ term by term in partial fractions:
    $\Phi(k) = \begin{bmatrix} 3(0.2)^k - 2(0.3)^k & -10(0.2)^k + 10(0.3)^k \\ 0.6(0.2)^k - 0.6(0.3)^k & -2(0.2)^k + 3(0.3)^k \end{bmatrix}$.
    At $k = 0$ this is $I$; at $k = 1$ it is $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -0.06 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix} = F$.
  5. For the first companion realization in problem 2, find the unit step response from $x(0) = 0$.
    Show solution
    $\dfrac{Y(z)}{z} = \dfrac{z + 0.5}{(z - 1)(z - 0.2)(z - 0.3)} = \dfrac{75/28}{z - 1} + \dfrac{35/4}{z - 0.2} - \dfrac{80/7}{z - 0.3}$, so $y(k) = \tfrac{75}{28} + \tfrac{35}{4}(0.2)^k - \tfrac{80}{7}(0.3)^k$: $\{0, 1, 2, 2.44, 2.60, \dots\} \to G(1) = 2.68$. Iterating $x(k+1) = Fx(k) + g$ gives the same numbers.
  6. Show that the change of state $x = Pw$ ($P$ invertible) changes neither the eigenvalues nor the transfer function.
    Show solution
    The new model is $w(k+1) = P^{-1}FPw(k) + P^{-1}gu(k)$, $y = cPw + du$. Then $|zI - P^{-1}FP| = |P^{-1}(zI - F)P| = |zI - F|$, and $cP(zI - P^{-1}FP)^{-1}P^{-1}g + d = cP\,P^{-1}(zI - F)^{-1}P\,P^{-1}g + d = c(zI - F)^{-1}g + d$. This is why one $G(z)$ has many state models, the canonical forms among them.
  7. Example 1 has two states but a first-order transfer function $2/(z - 3)$. Where did the second mode go, and does it still affect the states?
    Show solution
    The factor $(z - 1)$ cancels between numerator and denominator. The mode $1^k$ is still in $x_1(k)$ (it appears in the response from $x(0)$), but the output $y = x_2$ never sees it, because $x_2$ does not depend on $x_1$. A transfer function only shows the modes that are both excited by $u$ and seen at $y$; Lesson 7 makes this precise with controllability and observability.
