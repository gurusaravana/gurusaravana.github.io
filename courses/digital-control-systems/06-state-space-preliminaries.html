Notes

The next lesson designs controllers and observers on state models. That work rests on a handful of ideas from continuous-time state space: how to pick state variables, the standard canonical forms, the transfer function of a state model, controllability, observability, eigenvalues and changes of coordinates. This lesson reviews them for $\dot x = Ax + Bu$. Every result carries over to $x(k+1) = Fx(k) + gu(k)$ by replacing $s$ with $z$ and $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$ with $F$, $g$, $c$, $d$.

Why state space?

A transfer function is the ratio of output to input transforms with zero initial conditions. That makes it a compact tool for linear, time-invariant, single-input single-output systems, and also its limit.

Transfer function (classical control)State space (modern control)
single input, single outputmany inputs and outputs
linear onlylinear or nonlinear
time invariant onlytime invariant or time varying
initial conditions set to zeroinitial state is part of the model
input-output behaviour onlyinternal behaviour (hidden modes) visible

State variables and the state model

Example 1. The mass obeys $M\ddot y + B\dot y + Ky = u$, with $u = f(t)$ and $y$ the displacement from equilibrium. The equation is second order, so it needs two integrators and two state variables. Choose the position and the velocity:

$$x_1 = y, \quad x_2 = \dot y \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \dot x_1 = x_2, \quad \dot x_2 = -\frac{K}{M}x_1 - \frac{B}{M}x_2 + \frac{1}{M}u$$

$$\begin{bmatrix} \dot x_1 \\ \dot x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -K/M & -B/M \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1/M \end{bmatrix}u, \qquad y = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}$$

In general

$$\dot x(t) = Ax(t) + Bu(t) \quad\text{(state equation)}, \qquad y(t) = Cx(t) + Du(t) \quad\text{(output equation)}$$

Example 2. In the series RLC circuit, $v = Ri + L\,\dfrac{di}{dt} + v_C$ and $i = C\,\dfrac{dv_C}{dt}$. The energy stores give the states: $x_1 = i$ (inductor) and $x_2 = v_C$ (capacitor). With $u = v$ and $y = v_C$,

$$\begin{bmatrix} \dot x_1 \\ \dot x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -R/L & -1/L \\ 1/C & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 1/L \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}u, \qquad y = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}x$$

State variables are not unique. Positions and velocities, currents and voltages, or purely mathematical coordinates all work, as long as there are $n$ of them for an $n$th-order system and they are independent. Each choice gives a different $(A, B, C, D)$ for the same physical system.

Canonical forms

Canonical forms are standard state models read straight from the differential equation or transfer function. Each suits a particular design method; the dynamics (eigenvalues, transfer function) are the same in all of them. Start from

$$y^{(n)} + a_1y^{(n-1)} + \dots + a_{n-1}\dot y + a_ny = b_0u^{(n)} + b_1u^{(n-1)} + \dots + b_{n-1}\dot u + b_nu, \qquad \frac{Y(s)}{U(s)} = \frac{b_0s^n + b_1s^{n-1} + \dots + b_n}{s^n + a_1s^{n-1} + \dots + a_n}$$

Phase variable form

When no input derivatives appear, use the output and its derivatives as states: $x_1 = y$, $x_2 = \dot y$, …, $x_n = y^{(n-1)}$.

Example 3. $2y^{(3)} + 4\ddot y + 6\dot y + 8y = 10u$. Divide by 2 first: $y^{(3)} = -4y - 3\dot y - 2\ddot y + 5u$. Then

$$\dot x = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ -4 & -3 & -2 \end{bmatrix}x + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}u, \qquad y = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}x$$

Controllable canonical form

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & & & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 \\ -a_n & -a_{n-1} & -a_{n-2} & \cdots & -a_1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad C = \begin{bmatrix} b_n - a_nb_0 & \cdots & b_1 - a_1b_0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad D = b_0$$

This is the same structure as the first companion form of Lesson 5. For a strictly proper transfer function ($b_0 = 0$), $C = [b_n\ \cdots\ b_1]$.

Observable canonical form

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & -a_n \\ 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & -a_{n-1} \\ \vdots & & \ddots & & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 & -a_1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} b_n - a_nb_0 \\ \vdots \\ b_1 - a_1b_0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad C = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \cdots & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad D = b_0$$

It is the transpose (dual) of the controllable form: $A_o = A_c^T$, $B_o = C_c^T$, $C_o = B_c^T$.

Example 4. $\dfrac{Y(s)}{U(s)} = \dfrac{s + 3}{s^2 + 3s + 2}$ in controllable form: $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -2 & -3 \end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $C = [3\ \ 1]$.

Example 5. $\dfrac{Y(s)}{U(s)} = \dfrac{3s + 3}{s^3 + 2s^2 + s + 3}$, so $a_1 = 2$, $a_2 = 1$, $a_3 = 3$, $b_1 = 0$, $b_2 = 3$, $b_3 = 3$.

The slides write the observable form of Example 5 as $A = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 1 \\ -3 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $B = [0\ \ 3\ \ 3]^T$, $C = [1\ \ 0\ \ 0]$. That is also correct (it gives the same transfer function), but it numbers the states in the opposite order to the general formula on the previous slide. Reverse the order of the states and the two agree.

Diagonal and Jordan forms

If the poles $-p_1, \dots, -p_n$ are distinct, expand in partial fractions:

$$\frac{Y(s)}{U(s)} = b_0 + \frac{c_1}{s + p_1} + \frac{c_2}{s + p_2} + \dots + \frac{c_n}{s + p_n} \quad\Rightarrow\quad A = \operatorname{diag}(-p_1, \dots, -p_n), \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ \vdots \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad C = [c_1\ \cdots\ c_n], \quad D = b_0$$

Example 6. $\dfrac{s + 3}{(s + 1)(s + 2)} = \dfrac{2}{s + 1} - \dfrac{1}{s + 2}$, so $A = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $C = [2\ \ -1]$.

A repeated pole gives a Jordan block. For a triple pole at $-p_1$, $\dfrac{c_1}{(s + p_1)^3} + \dfrac{c_2}{(s + p_1)^2} + \dfrac{c_3}{s + p_1}$ is realized by

$$A_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -p_1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -p_1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & -p_1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad C_1 = [c_1\ \ c_2\ \ c_3]$$

and the distinct poles fill the rest of the diagonal as before.

From state space to transfer function

Laplace-transform both equations with zero initial state: $sX(s) = AX(s) + BU(s)$, so $X(s) = (sI - A)^{-1}BU(s)$, and $Y(s) = CX(s) + DU(s)$. Hence

$$\frac{Y(s)}{U(s)} = C(sI - A)^{-1}B + D$$

For several inputs and outputs this is a $q \times p$ transfer matrix.

Example 7. For the mass-spring-damper with the velocity as output, $K = 3$, $B = 1$, $M = 10$:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -0.3 & -0.1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0.1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad C = [0\ \ 1], \quad D = 0$$

$$(sI - A)^{-1} = \frac{1}{s^2 + 0.1s + 0.3}\begin{bmatrix} s + 0.1 & 1 \\ -0.3 & s \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \frac{Y(s)}{U(s)} = \frac{0.1s}{s^2 + 0.1s + 0.3} = \frac{s}{10s^2 + s + 3}$$

Eigenvalues

The eigenvalues of $A$ are the roots of the characteristic equation $|\lambda I - A| = 0$. They are the poles of the transfer function (unless a pole cancels, see below), so they fix stability and the speed of the response.

Example 8. $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ -6 & -11 & -6 \end{bmatrix}$: $|\lambda I - A| = \lambda^3 + 6\lambda^2 + 11\lambda + 6 = (\lambda + 1)(\lambda + 2)(\lambda + 3)$, eigenvalues $-1$, $-2$, $-3$.

Example 9. Mass-spring-damper with $K = 2$, $M = 10$, $B = 3$: $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -0.2 & -0.3 \end{bmatrix}$, $\lambda^2 + 0.3\lambda + 0.2 = 0$, $\lambda = -0.15 \pm j0.421$. The mass oscillates as it settles.

An eigenvector $p_i$ of $\lambda_i$ satisfies $Ap_i = \lambda_ip_i$, that is $(\lambda_iI - A)p_i = 0$. The equations are dependent, so $p_i$ is fixed only up to a non-zero scale factor.

Similarity transformations

Change coordinates with $x = T\bar x$, where $T$ is any non-singular $n \times n$ matrix. Substituting into the state model gives a new model of the same plant:

$$\dot{\bar x} = \bar A\bar x + \bar Bu, \quad y = \bar C\bar x + \bar Du, \qquad \bar A = T^{-1}AT, \quad \bar B = T^{-1}B, \quad \bar C = CT, \quad \bar D = D$$

A similarity transformation keeps everything that matters: $|sI - \bar A| = |sI - A|$, so the eigenvalues are unchanged, and $\bar C(sI - \bar A)^{-1}\bar B + \bar D = C(sI - A)^{-1}B + D$, so the transfer function is unchanged. The canonical forms are all similarity transforms of each other.

Transforming to diagonal form

If $A$ has $n$ independent eigenvectors, take them as the columns of $T$. Then $T^{-1}AT = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$. A diagonal state matrix decouples the states, and powers and exponentials of it are found entry by entry.

Example 10. $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ -1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $C = [1\ \ 1]$. Then $|\lambda I - A| = (\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 4) + 2 = \lambda^2 - 5\lambda + 6$, so $\lambda_1 = 2$, $\lambda_2 = 3$.

For $\lambda_1 = 2$: $-p_{11} + 2p_{12} = 0$, take $p_1 = [1\ \ \tfrac12]^T$. For $\lambda_2 = 3$: $-2p_{21} + 2p_{22} = 0$, take $p_2 = [-1\ \ -1]^T$.

$$T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ \tfrac12 & -1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad T^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -2 \\ 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \bar A = T^{-1}AT = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \bar B = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \bar C = \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac32 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$$

The slide prints $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ -1 & -4 \end{bmatrix}$, but every later step ($\lambda - 4$ in the determinant, eigenvalues 2 and 3, the eigenvectors and $\bar A$) belongs to $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ -1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$. With $-4$ the eigenvalues would be $0.56$ and $-3.56$.

Controllability

A system is completely state controllable if some unconstrained input $u(t)$ can move any initial state $x(t_0)$ to any desired state in finite time. The test uses the controllability matrix

$$\mathcal{C} = \begin{bmatrix} B & AB & A^2B & \cdots & A^{n-1}B \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \text{controllable} \iff \operatorname{rank}\mathcal{C} = n$$

For a single input, $\mathcal{C}$ is square and the test is $|\mathcal{C}| \ne 0$.

Example 11. $A = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -3 \end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $C = [1\ \ 2]$. $AB = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, so $\mathcal{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ has rank 1 < 2. Not controllable: the input never reaches $x_2$, whose mode $e^{-3t}$ just decays on its own.

Observability

A system is completely observable if the initial state $x(0)$ can be worked out from the output $y(t)$ (and the known input) over a finite interval. The test uses the observability matrix

$$\mathcal{O} = \begin{bmatrix} C \\ CA \\ CA^2 \\ \vdots \\ CA^{n-1} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \text{observable} \iff \operatorname{rank}\mathcal{O} = n$$

Example 12. $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $C = [0\ \ 4]$. $CA = [0\ \ -8]$, so $\mathcal{O} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 4 \\ 0 & -8 \end{bmatrix}$ has rank 1. Not observable: $y = 4x_2$ and $x_2$ does not depend on $x_1$, so $x_1(0)$ cannot be recovered.

The slide gives $CA = [0\ \ -12]$. The product is $[0 \cdot 0 + 4 \cdot 0\ \ \ 0 \cdot 1 + 4 \cdot (-2)] = [0\ \ -8]$. The conclusion (rank 1, not observable) is the same.

An uncontrollable or unobservable mode is exactly the one that cancels in $C(sI - A)^{-1}B$. In Example 11, $G(s) = \dfrac{1}{s + 1}$: the pole at $-3$ has gone. The transfer function only shows modes that are both controllable and observable, which is why state-space design checks both matrices first.

For the discrete model $x(k+1) = Fx(k) + gu(k)$, $y = cx$, the tests are identical with $F$, $g$, $c$: $\operatorname{rank}[g\ \ Fg\ \cdots\ F^{n-1}g] = n$ and $\operatorname{rank}[c;\ cF;\ \cdots;\ cF^{n-1}] = n$. Lesson 7 uses them.

Exercises

  1. Write the series RLC circuit (Example 2) with $R = 2\ \Omega$, $L = 1$ H, $C = 0.5$ F in state form and find $V_C(s)/V(s)$.
    Show solution
    $A = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & -1 \\ 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $C = [0\ \ 1]$. $|sI - A| = s^2 + 2s + 2$ and $C\operatorname{adj}(sI - A)B = 2$, so $\dfrac{V_C(s)}{V(s)} = \dfrac{2}{s^2 + 2s + 2} = \dfrac{1}{LCs^2 + RCs + 1}$, as circuit analysis gives.
  2. Find the transfer function of $\dot x = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ -1 & -2 & -3 \end{bmatrix}x + \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}u$, $y = [1\ \ 0\ \ 0]\,x$.
    Show solution
    $|sI - A| = s^3 + 3s^2 + 2s + 1$. Only the (1,1) entry of $\operatorname{adj}(sI - A)$ is needed, because $B$ has only a first entry and $C$ picks the first row: it is $s(s + 3) + 2 = s^2 + 3s + 2$. So $T(s) = \dfrac{10(s^2 + 3s + 2)}{s^3 + 3s^2 + 2s + 1}$.
  3. Give the controllable, observable and diagonal forms of $\dfrac{Y(s)}{U(s)} = \dfrac{2s + 5}{s^2 + 5s + 6}$.
    Show solution
    $a_1 = 5$, $a_2 = 6$, $b_1 = 2$, $b_2 = 5$; poles $-2$, $-3$.
    Controllable: $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -6 & -5 \end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $C = [5\ \ 2]$.
    Observable: $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -6 \\ 1 & -5 \end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$, $C = [0\ \ 1]$.
    Diagonal: $\dfrac{2s + 5}{(s + 2)(s + 3)} = \dfrac{1}{s + 2} + \dfrac{1}{s + 3}$, so $A = \operatorname{diag}(-2, -3)$, $B = [1\ \ 1]^T$, $C = [1\ \ 1]$.
  4. Check controllability and observability of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $C = [0\ \ 1]$.
    Show solution
    $AB = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $\mathcal{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, determinant $-1$: controllable. $CA = [-1\ \ 0]$, $\mathcal{O} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, determinant $1$: observable. (The answer is the same if the $-1$ in $A$ is $+1$.)
  5. Diagonalize $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -2 & -3 \end{bmatrix}$ and check $T^{-1}AT$.
    Show solution
    $\lambda^2 + 3\lambda + 2 = 0$: $\lambda = -1, -2$. For a companion matrix the eigenvector of $\lambda$ is $[1\ \ \lambda]^T$, so $T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$ (a Vandermonde matrix), $T^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ -1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $T^{-1}AT = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$.
  6. Show that the system of Example 11 has transfer function $1/(s + 1)$, and explain what happened to the eigenvalue $-3$.
    Show solution
    $(sI - A)^{-1} = \operatorname{diag}\!\left(\dfrac{1}{s + 1}, \dfrac{1}{s + 3}\right)$, so $C(sI - A)^{-1}B = [1\ \ 2]\begin{bmatrix} 1/(s + 1) \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \dfrac{1}{s + 1}$. The mode at $-3$ is not driven by the input (uncontrollable), so it cannot appear in the input-output relation. It still shows up in the response to a non-zero $x_2(0)$.
